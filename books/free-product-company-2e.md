## Chapter 2: What Is a Free / Negatively Priced Product?

What if a bottle of water didn't just cost you nothing — what if drinking it put money in your pocket, or sent clean water to a kid who needed it? Not as a onetime gimmick. Every single time. That's not a daydream. It's a kind of product that already exists, and this chapter is about what it is and how to think about it. Two ideas sit at the center of this entire book: free, and better than free. Get these two clear and everything else falls into place.

### Start with the map

Picture the number line from grade-school math. Zero in the middle. Everything to the right of zero costs money. Everything to the left of zero pays you back. Almost every product in human history lives on the right — you hand over money, you get a thing. Then, about twenty years ago, a handful of products reached zero: Google, YouTube, your email. Free. You pay nothing; someone else covers the cost. This book is about the part of the line almost nobody has built yet — the left side, where the product is free and then gives you something on top.

### Free, defined

Free is simple: $0 to the person receiving it. That's the whole definition. A free bottle of water costs the drinker nothing.

### Negatively priced, defined

This is where it gets interesting. A negatively priced product is free — and it comes with something extra. Better than free. That "extra" comes in two flavors: • •

A donation to a cause. Every unit sends money somewhere good — clean water, trees, meals. A direct payment or reward. The person literally gets something back — cash, credit, points, a prize entry.

The strongest products often stack both: free, plus a donation, plus a reward. We'll come back to that. Here's the whole thing on a napkin: FREE = $0 NEGATIVELY PRICED = Free + something extra (for example, -110%) That "-110%" is how the product feels to the person holding it. A paid bottle costs them, say, a dollar. A free bottle saves them that dollar — and then hands them another ten cents of value on top. In their eyes, the price didn't just hit zero. It went past it.

### The one test that actually matters

Now, a fair question — and the most important one in this chapter. A donation isn't cash in your pocket. So how is that "getting paid"? Here's the test you'll use on your own product for the rest of your career: when someone walks away from your product, do they feel they came out ahead? A free bottle that quenched their thirst and sent clean water to someone who needed it — most people walk away feeling richer for it, even though they never pocketed the dime. That feeling, not the math on a spreadsheet, is what makes something truly better than free. If your "extra" passes that test, you've built the real thing. If it doesn't, you haven't — no matter what the numbers say.

### Real free vs. fake free

Because here's the trap most people miss: not everything "free" is good for you. Facebook is free. Its parent company even gives money to charity. On paper, that looks like free-plus-a-donation. But think about how you feel walking away from an hour of it — the time gone, the data taken, the comparison and the noise. Most people don't feel they came out ahead. They feel the opposite. That's why people say, "If it's free, you're the product." A free bottle that hydrates you and funds clean water passes the test. Facebook doesn't. Same word — "free" — completely different thing. Your job as a builder is to stay on the right side of that line, always.

### How is this even possible?

So how can a product be free, fund a donation, and turn a profit? Because someone else pays for it — an advertiser, a sponsor, a brand. The ad on the package covers the whole chain: making it, moving it, the profit, the donation, all of it.

And there are only two dials you ever turn to push a product further past free: bring in money from new places (advertisers, sponsors, data), and cut waste out of how it's made and delivered. Pull both and they compound. We'll spend whole chapters on each later — for now, just keep the two dials in your head.

### How far left can it go?

FreeWater's first campaign landed at -110% — free water plus a ten-cent donation. Later, a campaign with MrBeast reached -130%. But here's the thing: that's not the ceiling. That's just where the early, scrappy version stopped. The deeper, more carefully engineered models go much, much further — because a single product isn't limited to one ad. It can carry several. Its QR code can earn money every time someone scans it, sometimes months after they got it. One physical object becomes many small revenue events. The product has a ceiling; the participation around it doesn't. Want proof it can run deep? Look at Ecosia — a free search engine, like Google, funded entirely by ads. It has used that revenue to plant more than 200 million trees. No bottle. No packaging. Just the same underlying truth, running so far left of zero that the value it creates dwarfs the cost of the service many times over. The constraint was never money. It was design.

### Why advertisers love it

Why would an advertiser pay for all this? Because it works better than what they're doing now. Their message lands in someone's hands instead of getting skipped, scrolled past, or blocked — and it arrives attached to a gift, which makes people feel good about the brand instead of annoyed by it. It's the best brand-loyalty platform on earth. It also changes how you compete. On the right side of zero, you win by charging less. On the left, you can't — you can't charge less than free. So you win by giving more. That single shift is what makes this a movement instead of a marketing trick.

### The ladder

Think of it like climbing a ladder:

- 

A free bottle of water is impressive. A free bottle of water + 10¢ to charity is unforgettable. A free bottle + a donation + a reward + a lottery entry is unstoppable. And a free bottle that's printed just for you, scannable for an offer, and delivered to your door? That's a product the giants would have to rebuild themselves to match.

Every rung adds value the person didn't expect — and every rung pulls you further left of zero. One honest note before we move on, because people always ask: can't a huge company just copy this? They can. And I hope they do — a billion-dollar brand giving its products away for free would do enormous good in the world, and the whole point of open-sourcing this is to make that happen. But there's a difference between copying it and doing it at the highest efficiency. A giant can slap an ad on a bottle and hand it out tomorrow. What it can't do quickly is reach the deepest, most negatively priced version — the one with consecutive delivery, individualized packaging, owned production, and stacked revenue layers — because that version requires building entirely new infrastructure designed around the left side of zero. Their factories, their trucks, their whole machine were built to sell. Rebuilding all of that takes years. That gap — the years it takes them to rebuild — is your head start. And if they close it and join you anyway, you didn't lose. The mission won. So, whenever you hear "negatively priced," remember: Free is just the doorway. What makes this powerful is everything waiting on the other side of it.

## Chapter 3: The 10% Rule

Start with one bottle. Someone takes it on a hot afternoon, drinks it, and walks on. They didn't donate anything. They didn't fill out a form or round up at a register or sit through a sad commercial. They just drank water. And a dime went toward clean water for someone across the world anyway. That's the whole idea in miniature. Now let's scale it.

The 10% Rule is simple: if you get 10% of a population to use your negatively priced product every day, and each unit gives back a little, you create a massive river of donations — automatically. Nobody has to be generous. The giving is built into the product itself. Here's what makes that more than a slogan: you don't need 10% to start doing real good. You need it to do staggering good. The climb there is a staircase, and every step funds something real.

### Watch the number climb

Take FreeWater. Each bottle gives ten cents to fight the global water crisis. Watch what happens as it scales: • • • •

1,000 bottles a day → about $36,500 a year — roughly enough to fund a clean-water well outright. 100,000 a day → $3.65 million a year. 1 million a day → $36.5 million a year. 33 million a day — that's 10% of the United States — → over $1.2 billion a year.

The billion doesn't stay an abstraction: At ten cents a bottle, roughly every 150 FreeWater funds clean water for one person for life. The dime isn't a rounding error. It has a face. Every sip is a fraction of a human being's lifetime access to water. Notice what the staircase is telling you. Even a thousand bottles a day — a tiny, scrappy operation — funds a well a year. You don't have to reach the summit to matter. The summit is just the same engine, running bigger.

### Why it's automatic

This is what separates the 10% Rule from every charity model that came before it. The old way of giving must ask. It runs the galas, sends the emails, leans on guilt, and burns a chunk of every dollar on the asking itself. It fights donor fatigue. It stops when the campaign ends.

The 10% Rule never asks. The person was going to drink water anyway. The giving rides along for free, on autopilot, every single time, forever. You're not bolting generosity onto people's good intentions — you're baking it into a product they already wanted. Every scan, every sip, every use is a donation, whether the person is thinking about it or not.

### This works for any product — even a paid one

Now, an obvious question: couldn't a can of Coke do this too? Donate a dime a can, sell billions, fund a fortune? Yes. The math is identical. And that points at something worth understanding. A company like Coca-Cola or Pepsi could have donated a dime a can a decade ago. They didn't — not because they're villains, but because of where the dime comes from. On a product you sell, every dime you give comes straight out of your profit. It's a cost. And a cost gets defended: kept small, run as a limited-time campaign, quietly dropped when the quarter looks tight. That's why causemarketing donations tend to be tiny and temporary. The structure pushes them that way. On a negatively priced product, the dime doesn't come out of your margin. It's funded by how the system is built — and that can be advertising, but it can just as easily be efficiency, sponsorship, donated supplies, or volunteer labor. The giving is part of the engine, not subtracted from it. That's why it can be permanent and large instead of occasional and token. And hold onto this, because people assume otherwise: You can build a negatively priced model with ads or without them. Advertising is the most common on-ramp — it's the easiest lever for a newcomer with a phone and a few hundred dollars. But a nonprofit running on donated product and volunteers can give everything away and fund a cause without selling a single ad. More ways to fund the give means less friction. That's the structural advantage of operating left of zero: a paid product has exactly one place to find the dime — its own margin — and you have many.

### Now zoom out

FreeWater is one product. Apply the same math to food, hygiene products, soap, diapers — the hundreds of everyday things every human touches. Reach 10% of the planet — nearly 800 million people — across a basket of negatively priced essentials, and you're no longer talking about a billion dollars. You're talking about hundreds of billions flowing every year, passively, permanently, without a single gala or government approval. Not because anyone gave generously. Because the giving was designed into things people use anyway.

### What has to be true

Now let me be straight with you, because a number that big deserves honesty. The river doesn't flow on its own. Three things have to be true: •

- 

You have to reach people, every single day. A donation per unit means nothing if the units don't move. Distribution is the whole game, and most of this book is about it. The unit economics have to clear. Remember the two dials from the last chapter — new revenue in, waste out. The give only works if the system funds it. If your model doesn't clear, the donation isn't generosity; it's a hole in the boat. The giving has to be verifiable. People have to trust the dime really moved. A donation no one can confirm isn't impact — it's a marketing claim, and the difference matters enormously.

Get those three right and the math becomes inevitable. Get them wrong and the billion stays a daydream. The rule is real. It just isn't free of work.

### How much to give

So how much should you donate per unit? Tier it to the market you're in: • • • •

High-income countries: at least $0.10 per unit. Middle-income countries: around $0.05 per unit. Lower-income regions: $0.02 per unit still goes a long way. Nonprofits may choose to give 100% of profit to a cause.

These aren't random. A dime carries a different weight in different economies, and matching the give to the market keeps your impact real and your model alive in both Lagos and Los Angeles. And here's the reframe that matters: On a product you sell, a donation is a tax — it comes out of your margin. On a negatively priced product, it isn't, because the architecture pays it, not you. So don't think of the give as a cost you're tolerating. Think of it as the engine. It's the very thing that makes your product pass the test from the last chapter — the reason the person walks away feeling they came out ahead. Take the donation away and you don't have a better product. You have an ordinary one.

### The payoff

When you build it right, giving stops being something you do after profit and becomes the thing that drives it. It's your moat — hard to match without rebuilding. It's your growth engine — people share what makes them feel good. And it's your legacy — a river of impact that keeps flowing long after you've moved on. But all of it depends on the one thing the staircase quietly assumed: reaching those people in the first place. That's what the rest of this book is for. This was one product and one dime. Now let's build the machine that puts it into the world.

## Chapter 4: The Startup Reality Check

Let me save you from the most expensive misunderstanding I see, over and over. People assume launching a free or negatively priced product is like launching an app or a drop-shipping store: build a website, flip a switch, go. It isn't. You are making a physical thing that has to be manufactured, moved, and placed into a real human's hand — and then a service layered on top of it. That means real logistics, real manufacturing, real costs, and real-world problems that no amount of clever code makes disappear. This is harder and more real than software. I'd rather you know that on page one than learn it the painful way like I did.

Now the relief: It's cheaper and more doable than you think, because I've already spent the years and the money proving the model works. You're not pioneering. You're executing. But before you spend a dollar, make one decision that matters more than your budget.

### First, pick your vehicle

If you're an experienced founder with capital and connections, build a for-profit company — go for it. But if this is your first time, or you're starting with very little, hear me out: A nonprofit is the more forgiving way to begin. Not a lesser way. A smarter on-ramp. Here's why it forgives the exact mistakes beginners make. On a for-profit, you have to fund your donation out of a margin you haven't figured out yet — a nonprofit can give 100% of profit to the cause and skip that whole headache on day one. Manufacturers can legally donate surplus product and capacity to a nonprofit for the tax write-off, so the thing that wrecks most first-timers — manufacturing minimums, cost per unit — can come in near zero. Volunteers will distribute for a mission, so you can field a team you couldn't otherwise afford. Grants, tax-deductible donations, and sponsorships-with-a-write-off are all funding sources that don't demand you already have traction. And people forgive a scrappy nonprofit; the same wobbly launch from a business just reads as a struggling business. Notice the pattern: Every one of those perks is also a resource you didn't have to pay for — donated bottles, free hands, free space, easier money. Which means a nonprofit can run leaner than a for-profit doing the exact same thing, because it can pull in help from places a business legally can't. There's a name for that — aggregate efficiency, getting more output from fewer resources — and it's the engine the second book is built on. For now, just know the nonprofit structure gives you a higher possible ceiling on it. Possible, not guaranteed: The advantage is sitting there in the structure, but only if you actually go pull those levers. Be clear-eyed about the trade-off, because it's real: a nonprofit isn't something you own or can sell, you can't raise venture money on normal terms, and you can't turn it into personal wealth. It also comes with a board, reporting, and compliance — different paperwork, not less.

But here's the part most people miss: The nonprofit isn't a ceiling. It's a launchpad. Once you've built a successful one, you can go start a for-profit arm, a spin-off, or a sister company — and you'll do it carrying a proven model, manufacturer relationships that already trust you, advertisers who've already seen the medium work, an audience and a brand, real data about what people actually want, and operating skill you earned where mistakes were cheap. You will have de-risked the for-profit by running the nonprofit first. That's not a consolation prize. For someone starting with no experience and no capital, it might be the single smartest sequence there is. (One caution: How assets, brand, and relationships can and can't move between a nonprofit and a for-profit varies by country and has real legal and tax lines. This isn't legal advice — when you get there, hire a lawyer and an accountant. Don't improvise it.) So, the choice tracks your goal. Want to build equity you own and you've got the experience? For-profit. Want the most forgiving path to impact and speed, with a launchpad to a business later? Start as a nonprofit.

### The good news on money

With the right execution, people have gotten their first water campaign off the ground for as little as $500 to $5,000, depending on goals, country, and resourcefulness. The money goes to roughly three things: bottles, label printing, and a cheap domain. That's basically it. Where you are changes the math. In India, Pakistan, or parts of Africa, the bottom of that range is completely real. In the US or Europe, you'll trade money for hustle — you'll need to work harder and lean on relationships — but it's still very possible. Here's how.

### The goal of your first run

Before the steps, get this straight: The goal of your first batch is feedback on the concept — not a perfect product. You are running an experiment, not opening a business. Everything below is designed to get a real product into real hands so real people can react. Judge yourself on what you learn, not on how polished it looks.

### Step 1: Build a simple, professional website

You need a site that makes a stranger get it fast. If you like the look of www.freewater.io, copy the structure — never the name, logo, or content — and make it your own. You only need four pages: •

- 

Everyone's Journey — a mission page that gets people emotionally aligned with the vision and signs up to get free products in the future. Inspire, educate, plant the big idea. Advertiser Page — the path for sponsors. Spell out what they get, why this ad medium works, and how to start. Add a simple lead form. Distributor Page — for the people who'll help hand out your product. Keep it simple and inviting. Contact Us — a good photo and a basic form. Nothing fancy.

The point isn't to look like a tech unicorn. It's to make it instantly obvious what this is and what the visitor should do next. You nailed it if: a stranger lands on your site and within ten seconds knows what this is, that it's real, and exactly what they do next — sign up, advertise, help distribute, or reach you.

### Step 2: Make your first prototype (the safe way)

Remember the goal: You need something real enough that people can react to it. There's a ladder of ways to get there, and I want you to start at the top, not the bottom. Safest and fastest: Design a bold label and print it as a mockup — a wrap or a sheet you can hold up, or slip over a bottle, to show people the concept. You can collect honest reactions to a great-looking mockup without manufacturing anything. Still simple: Buy your own bottles legally, and apply your own clearly marked sleeve or sticker over them, labeled plainly as a concept test from your brand. Now you're holding a real, brandable prototype you can hand out.

Whatever you make, design the label like a magazine cover — bold, artistic, attention-grabbing. Do not make it look like ordinary bottled-water branding. The whole point is that people want to be seen holding it. Now, the scrappier version, as honesty rather than instruction: In the early days I made prototypes by removing labels from store-bought drinks and re-wrapping them — and I'll tell you plainly that altering and redistributing commercial products is not legal in most countries, so I'm not telling you to do it. My own first "product" was unrefrigerated cans of beer I re-wrapped and handed to people stuck in line outside a supermarket in the first days of the pandemic, just to collect reactions before I knew any of what's in this book. It worked as a feedback tool. It was also not something I'd hand a beginner as a plan. Use the safer options above; they get you the same learning without the exposure. You nailed it if: your prototype looks like something people genuinely want to hold and show off — and it's clearly presented as your concept, not a disguise.

### Step 3: Hit the streets

Grab a couple of friends, dress sharp, and head to a busy part of your city. Look professional — you're representing a real idea. Offer the bottles for free, one person at a time. Before you hand one over, ask: "Would you mind making a quick 20-second video for social media in exchange for a free bottle of water?" Most people will say no. That's fine. If 1 in 10 says yes, you've got gold. You nailed it if: people stop, smile, and ask you a question back — "wait, why is it free?" Polite take-and-walk means your pitch or your packaging isn't landing yet.

### Step 4: Your questions are the engine

This is the most important part of the whole chapter, so slow down here. You are not filming an ad. You are filming a person discovering something. That distinction is everything, because discovery is what spreads. When a viewer watches a stranger's genuine surprise at free water, they don't think "I'm being sold to" — they picture themselves in that moment. And because the thing is free, the takeaway isn't "buy this," it's "this exists, and I could have it too." That's a far more contagious message than any ad, and it's why your scrappy street footage can outperform a polished commercial. So, your job is to draw out real reactions. Have 10–20 great questions ready and memorized, and know your own answers cold, because they'll ask back. Sort them by what each one does: Open them up (warm, easy): • •

What's the most you've ever paid for a bottle of water? Have you ever seen packaging like this before?

Make them think (the good stuff): • • •

Do you think water should be free? Do you know why this water is free? Why do you think I gave it to you for free?

Get the quotable line (the spark): • •

If this water can be free, what else could be? What would you do if your groceries were free?

That last kind is what you're hunting for — the moment someone's face changes as the idea lands. That's the clip that travels. Film vertically. Keep the sun or lights behind you so faces are lit. Get one person, somewhere not too loud, phone held steady. Smile, bring real energy, and actually love what you're doing — it shows on camera. And always ask permission to post. One more: Don't reinvent your content style from scratch. Study the videos that already perform — ours and others' — and model the pacing, length, and tone. Then make your version. Authenticity beats polish every time. You nailed it if: watching the clip back, you can feel the person's real surprise — and one clip clearly outperforms the rest.

## Chapter 5: The Two Types of Free Products

Put two free products on a table. On the left, a free bottle of water. On the right, a free can of Coke. To the person taking them, they're identical — both cost nothing. But underneath, they're funded by two completely different machines. And if you don't know which machine you're building, you'll waste months trying to power one with the wrong engine. There are two ways to make a product free or negatively priced. Get this distinction clear and you'll know, for any product on earth, exactly how to fund it. (A quick note for anyone who read the first edition: I used to call these BVRE and AVRE. Same two ideas — clearer names.)

### Type 1: Embedded Value — the product pays for itself

The first type is the simplest, and it's where you should start. Embedded Value means the product generates enough value just by being what it is to cover its entire cost. The ad printed on the packaging is worth more than what it costs to make, ship, and hand out the product — enough to cover all of that, fund a donation, and still leave a profit. In other words: The packaging alone pays for everything. No app required. No digital layer. No software team. The product funds itself the moment it's made. These are the products to start with. They're simple, cheap, and their packaging is valuable ad space — especially at scale: Bottled water, soda, beer, rice, beans, flour, canned food, fruits and vegetables, chips, cereal, cooking oil, spices, toiletries, diapers, soap, paper towels, toilet paper — and even some modes of transportation, like bikes, tuk-tuks, taxis, buses, and trains.

What they share: low cost to produce, and a surface big or frequent enough that an advertiser will pay more for the exposure than the whole product costs to deliver.

### The proof, on one bottle

Let me show you the entire model on a single product, with real numbers and nothing hidden. Here's a 473ml aluminum bottle of FreeWater: • • • •

Cost to manufacture: about $0.80 Donation built in: $0.10 Ad revenue per bottle: about $1.15+ Minimum order: 1 pallet (1,680 bottles)

Now watch what scale does to the cost. Order 500,000 units and manufacturing drops to about $0.50 a bottle. Order a few million and it drops another nickel. At Coca-Cola, Pepsi, or Nestlé scale, that same aluminum bottle costs roughly $0.20 to produce — and that's a number those companies already hit today. The paper carton tells the same story: about $0.40 to make at small scale, $1.00– $1.30 in ad revenue, dropping toward $0.10–$0.15 per unit at major-brand scale. And all of this is through outside co-packers making the product for us. If you owned your own factory, it would be cheaper still. Here's the punchline, and it's the proof of this entire book: The ad on the packaging generates roughly twice the cost of the whole supply chain — and still funds a donation and a profit. Not in theory. Right now. Even at low order quantities. One honest caveat, because every real number deserves it: These figures move with scale, your supplier, your country, and the year you're reading this. Treat them as a real example, not a quote. Run your own numbers for your own product. (A note on materials: this would be even easier in plastic or reusable glass. We started with aluminum and paper because they were strong entry points, we refused to put free water in plastic, and reusable glass barely exists in the US today. More on that trade-off in the next chapter.)

### Type 2: Generated Value — the user creates the value

Now the second type. Some products can't pay for themselves through their packaging — and there are two different reasons why, which most people confuse. Reason one: The product costs too much, or its surface is too small. A fifteen-dollar bottle of wine can't generate fifteen dollars from a label. Expensive makeup is too small to advertise on. Designer goods, workout equipment, certain electronics — the packaging can't carry enough value to cover the cost. Reason two (this one's subtler): The brand owns its own surface. Think about a can of Coke. The economics would work fine; a well-placed ad could easily cover a can of soda. The problem isn't cost. The problem is that Coke's can is Coke's ad. Pepsi can't buy that space. The surface belongs to the brand and always will. The same is true of a McDonald's wrapper, a branded Chipotle bottle, an Uber ride in an Uber-branded car. Whenever a product's packaging is already its own marketing, you simply can't sell that space to a third party — no matter how well the math would work. Either way, the fix is the same. You make these products free through Generated Value — value the user creates through participation, then applies to the product. Here's how it works in its simplest form. You watch six short ads on your phone and earn $1 in tokens. You walk into a store — or shop online — and spend those tokens anywhere digital payment is accepted. You hand over $1 in tokens for a $1 Pepsi. The advertiser paid for your soda, through the ads you watched before you ever walked in. Your attention became the bridge. That same bridge can fund the wine, the makeup, the can of Coke — anything whose packaging can't or won't pay for itself. The value just gets generated somewhere else and carried over.

### You can stack them

These aren't either/or. The most powerful products use both. A bottle of water starts as Embedded Value — the label pays for it. Add a QR code that earns when it's scanned, and an affiliate link that earns when someone buys through it, and now the same bottle is funded by Embedded Value (the packaging) plus Generated Value (the scan and the purchase). What was already profitable gets more profitable, and the donation can grow. Stacking is how you go from a simple free bottle to the deep negative pricing we talked about back in Chapter 2. Each layer pushes the same product further left of zero.

### So, which should you start with?

Start with Embedded Value. A simple, low-cost product where the packaging alone pays — water, rice, soap. The reason is practical: Embedded Value needs no app, no developer, no software at all. You can launch it with the bottles in your hands and the lessons in this book. Generated Value is more powerful — it can make almost anything free, and because it's mostly software, it can scale in ways physical goods can't. But it's harder to build, and it usually needs a real development team. So, earn your traction with Embedded Value first, then reach for Generated Value when you've got the momentum or the developer to build it. And here's the tailwind: As AI and automation keep driving down costs, more and more products are sliding into reach of both types. The set of things you can make free is growing every year. Two products on a table, both free. Now you know there are two different engines under the hood — which one each product needs, and which one to build first. Next, let's figure out how valuable your product's ad space actually is, because not every free product reaches everyone — and not every impression is worth the same.

## Chapter 6: Choosing Your Product as an Ad Medium

Here's something that trips up almost every new founder: they assume that because a product is free, everyone will want it and everyone will see the ad. Not true — and it shouldn't be. A free bottle of water reaches the whole world. A free box of adult diapers reaches a specific person in a specific season of life. They're different ad mediums, worth different amounts to different advertisers. Your job is to know exactly what kind of medium your product is — so you can put the right brand on it and charge what it's worth. This chapter is the tool for that. There's one principle underneath everything here: Your ad space is worth: How many people see it × who those people are × how many times they see it. Reach, audience, and frequency. Get a realistic read on those three and you can price your ad space with confidence and close deals without guessing. Let's take them one at a time.

### Reach and audience: Who's really seeing it?

Some products reach everyone. Some reach a narrow, valuable few. Both can win — as long as you match the brand to the crowd. Water is the universal medium. It doesn't matter your age, income, religion, gender, politics, or diet — everyone drinks water. You can hand it out at offices, schools, festivals, disaster zones, city streets. That universality is exactly why we started with it: almost any advertiser fits. Diapers are the opposite, and that's a strength, not a weakness. A free diaper reaches one tightly defined, incredibly valuable audience. For baby diapers, you're not advertising to the baby — you're advertising to a parent, so the ad is toys, parenting apps, life insurance, baby gear. For adult diapers, you're reaching seniors and their families, so it's medical devices, retirement services, health supplements. Narrow audience, but a narrow audience is often worth more per impression, because the advertiser knows exactly who they're reaching. And then there's toilet paper — the awkward one with an angle. It can't hold a high-res ad, and let's be honest, most brands don't want their logo used to wipe. But that's not a dead end — it's an opening for the brands willing to have fun with it: baby wipes, satire, political jabs, a rival placement (a Pepsi ad on

Coke-branded TP?), or a QR code or joke people scan while they're, well, sitting there. Not for every advertiser. Perfect for the right one. Three products, three completely different mediums — universal, niche-butprecious, and oddball. The point isn't to memorize my examples. It's to look at your product and ask: who ends up holding this, and which brands would pay to reach exactly those people?

### Frequency: How many times will your ad be seen?

This is where most founders leave money on the table, because they price for one impression when their product delivers fifty. Here's the rule that changes everything: The longer and the more visibly your product lives in someone's life, the more times your ad is seen — and the more your ad space is worth. More impressions means more value means higher ad pricing. So, before you price anything, figure out how many times your ad will be seen. Three things drive it. The material — will it be reused, or thrown away? This one's dramatic with bottles. A plastic bottle gets used once and tossed — cheap and disposable. An aluminum bottle gets reused a few times, and people hold onto the cool-looking ones. A glass bottle gets reused the most — it feels premium, and people keep it for weeks. And a painted glass bottle goes furthest of all: Because the ad is printed right onto the glass, it can't be peeled off, so it keeps advertising on a desk, in a kitchen, even reused as a flower vase or an olive-oil dispenser. The trade-off: painted glass usually needs huge orders and can't carry high-res photos like a label can. Higher quality and longer exposure, or flexibility and lower cost — you choose based on what matters most. The use case — where does it live? A product that sits out on a counter delivers far more impressions than one tossed after a single use. A bottle of cooking oil can sit on a kitchen counter for months, seen hundreds of times. Soap dispensers live on sinks for weeks. Spices, salt, and pepper get used constantly but replaced slowly. Laundry detergent sits by the machine, seen by the whole household, over and over. Even size matters: a 5kg bag of rice is around far longer, and far more visible, than a 500g bag. The culture — how do these particular people use it? The same product delivers wildly different frequency depending on who receives it. In a country where people cook every day, free cooking oil and spices get used up fast. In a place like the US, where people eat out a lot, those same products might sit for years. So, when you estimate impressions, picture the actual household: How do people in this region use this item? Will it be reused? Will it be kept somewhere visible? You don't need perfect data — even an educated guess about reach, audience, and frequency lets you price smarter and walk into advertiser conversations with confidence instead of hope.

### The impression that's worth the most: the emotional one

Now the part that separates this from any billboard or banner ad. It's not only how often your product is seen — it's how it makes people feel every time. When your product is free and gives back to a cause people care about, every sighting sparks a small emotional response. The person remembers: I didn't pay a cent for this. A sponsor covered it — and gave to something that matters — on my behalf. That builds gratitude, loyalty, and a real emotional connection, not just to the product, but to the brand that made it possible. A billboard can't do that. Your free product does it every single day, on the counter, in the hand, at the desk.

### This is the future of brand loyalty

Picture a dinner table a few years from now where every drink, snack, cereal box, loaf of bread, and piece of chicken on it is free — all covered by advertisers. Say Nike sponsored most of it. When it's time to buy your kid new shoes, are you reaching for Adidas? Or for the brand that's been feeding your family? You'd want to return the favor. Now change the scene. Some of the food was made free by Nike — but the rest was negatively priced by Reebok. Reebok didn't just give your family something for free; they gave more. They paid you or gave to a cause you care about. Now who are you choosing? The brand that gave the most. The one visibly trying hardest to make a difference.

That's next-level brand loyalty. Not bought with a catchy jingle or a flashy commercial — earned through real impact and a daily reminder of it. It's also the competition idea from Chapter 2, made personal: On the left side of zero, brands don't win by shouting louder. They win by giving more. And your product is the place that competition plays out, right there on the kitchen table.

### The point

You don't need every product to reach everyone. You need to know what each product is — its reach, its audience, its frequency, and the feeling it carries — and then match the right brands to it. Universal products like water take almost any advertiser. Niche products like diapers command a premium from the few brands that fit. Reuse, visibility, and culture decide how often your ad is seen. And the donation makes every one of those impressions land with gratitude instead of annoyance. Get that right, and you're not selling ad space. You're selling the most loyal, most emotional, most-seen advertising medium a brand can buy. ## Chapter 7: How to Make Anything Free

You already know how to make water free. Now here's the bigger promise: The same model works on food, transportation, coffee, seeds — far more products than most people imagine. But I'm not going to hand you another list of examples and hope you connect the dots. I'm going to hand you the recipe — the exact questions you ask of any product to figure out whether it can be made free — and then show you that recipe at work on a handful of products, each one teaching a new move. Learn the recipe, and the title of this chapter becomes directionally true: far more products can be made free than you'd ever guess.

### The recipe: five questions

Run any product through these five questions, in order:

- 

- 

Embedded or Generated Value? Is the ad space on the physical product itself worth more than everything it costs — to make, ship, donate, and profit? That's Embedded: the real estate pays it, with no action from anyone. Or do you need people to watch, scan, or do something digital to fund it? That's Generated. The simple test: does the object do the work, or does the user do the work? (Embedded is the physical one — the value is embedded in the packaging and surfaces, not in any software.) What surfaces can carry value? Don't think "the label." Think every surface: the packaging, the box it ships in, the vehicle, the machine, the cup, the QR code, the app, even the minutes someone spends with it. Who sees it, and how often? Reach × audience × frequency, from Chapter 6. This tells you how valuable the ad space is and which brands to put on it. What has to be controlled? Age, safety, identity, regulation. Some products need gates. Figure out which before you launch, not after. Stack the layers until it crosses zero. Add surfaces — the physical Embedded base first, then Generated layers on top — until the value covers the cost, the donation, and a profit.

One thing before we go further, and it matters: The recipe doesn't mean every product should be made free. It means every product can be tested against the same design logic. Some products pass and should be built. Some pass on paper but shouldn't be pushed. The five questions are a diagnostic, not a command. Everything below is that recipe, demonstrated. The examples run from the simplest starter products to the advanced and regulated ones — don't treat them as equally easy. Watch how each adds a new tool to your kit.

### Seeds — a starter product that delivers itself, and multiplies

We've been asked to give away free vegetable seeds more times than I can count, and it's easy to see why. Seeds are cheap, they weigh almost nothing, and they ship in a plain envelope with a single stamp. Run the recipe: they're Embedded (the ads printed on the packet and the envelope are worth more than the tiny cost), the surfaces are small, but the cost is smaller, and the stamp handles distribution. But seeds teach a move nothing else on this list can. The packaging is the delivery system — the envelope that carries the ad is the shipping — which makes seeds maybe the cheapest free product to distribute at scale. And then the part that's almost magical: seeds multiply. One small pack can feed a family for a season or start a community garden that feeds dozens. Most free products hand someone value. Seeds help them create it. When you can find a product that produces more of itself, you've found something special.

### Coffee and EV charging — dwell time is premium ad space

Free coffee is easy — put ads on the cups and you can give it away profitably. But that's a thin version. The better version teaches a principle hiding inside EV charging too, and it's the cleanest example of stacking an Embedded base with a Generated layer. A free-coffee vending machine earns from every surface at once. The ads printed on the machine, the cups, and the lids are Embedded — that real estate pays for the coffee on its own. Then the ads someone watches on the screen, or in an app, while the drink brews are Generated — the user's attention, on top. An EV charger does the identical thing: Embedded ads on the charger body, Generated ads on the dashboard while the car charges, plus a kiosk of free products beside it, all powered cheaply by solar or wind. Why do both work so well? Dwell time. People stand still at a coffee machine for ninety seconds and at an EV charger for ten to thirty minutes — and captive, standing-still attention is the perfect moment to add a Generated layer on top of your Embedded base. So, when you're sizing up a product, ask: Does someone have to wait while they use this? If yes, that waiting is a goldmine. The charger isn't just giving away electricity — it's a hub for ads, commerce, and sustainability, and the dwell time is what makes it pay.

### Transportation — the product becomes a moving medium

What if getting around cost nothing? Bikes, scooters, tuk-tuks, buses, even cars — you can make them free by turning the ride itself into ad space. This example teaches that the product doesn't have to hold the ad. The product can be the ad. Put ads on a bike's frame, wheels, and basket, add GPS so advertisers can target specific neighborhoods, and every ride becomes a moving billboard — that's Embedded value on wheels. Then layer Generated value on top: Let people watch ads to earn rides (their attention pays for the trip) or go further with move-to-earn — reward distance with credits, or pair an exercise bike with impressions so riding itself becomes currency. The same playbook runs on scooters, buses, delivery vans, and drones. Transportation doesn't have to be a cost. Build the ad layer in, and brands fund the whole thing.

### Restricted products: age, safety, and legal gates

This section is about a category — any product the law puts controls around — and alcohol and cannabis are just the clearest examples. The lesson is the gating, not the goods. First, the responsibility, because it leads here, it doesn't trail. Restricted products must never be targeted at minors, never be glamorized, and never be pushed aggressively just because they can be free. Not everyone drinks or uses cannabis — some people struggle with addiction, others abstain for health, religious, or personal reasons, and that's completely valid. If you can't build it with real controls and real care, don't build it. Full stop. This is advanced, regulated territory — not where a first timer should start. With that as the floor: Legal beer and cannabis are two of the highest-turnover industries on the planet, and the recipe applies cleanly. Run it. They're Embedded: a can or bottle wrapped in printed ads; six- and twelve-packs as adrich boxes; for cannabis, pre-rolls or gummies in an ad-covered case dropped into an ad-covered bag — two full layers of packaging. Stack Generated layers on top: QR codes that open offers, trigger AR, lead to affiliate stores, enter lotteries, or push local dispensary and brewery discounts. The real tool here is question four — control — and it transfers to any gated product, from nicotine to certain medicines. A verification flow that works: the user downloads your app or visits your site and scans a government ID; facial recognition or video confirms the match before they order; at pickup or delivery, they scan the ID again. That's no different from how legal dispensaries and alcohol-delivery services already operate — you're just layering it onto a free platform. Solve verification once, and you can apply the model anywhere the law sets an age. The scale is real, too. Take Germany — 83 million people, serious beer culture. Same 10% Rule from Chapter 3, new product: If 10% drink one free beer a day with a €0.10 donation, that's €830,000 a day — over €300 million a year. (That's an illustration of the math, not a guarantee — run your own numbers for your own market.) Add cannabis where it's legal, scale across countries, and the passive-donation potential is enormous — from products that already have massive daily demand.

### When this is hard — and when it shouldn't be free at all

The recipe makes a lot possible, but be honest about the edges, because it'll save you months. Some products fight the model. Tiny surfaces — a single tampon, a lipstick — can't carry much of an Embedded ad, so they lean on Generated value: vending machines with screens, an app, a donated-and-volunteer nonprofit setup. (That small-surface playbook lives in Chapter 5 and the vending chapter; reach for it here.) Low-frequency products used once a year don't generate the repeated impressions advertisers pay for. Products whose buyers have no advertiser appeal produce thin ad value no matter how clever the packaging. And some categories — medicine is the clearest — carry safety, liability, and legal weight far beyond free coffee; treat anything like that as advanced and theoretical, not a weekend project, and not without real expertise. There's also a line that isn't about economics at all: Some things can be made free but shouldn't be pushed. The test from Chapter 2 still rules — does the person genuinely come out ahead? If making something free just gets more of it into hands that are worse off for it, the model is being misused. Build for real benefit, with real controls, or don't build it.

### Now you try

Here's how you'll know the recipe is yours: pick a product that isn't in this chapter and run the five questions on it. Free sunscreen at a beach? The ads on the bottle and the dispenser are Embedded — if that real estate covers the cost, the donation, and a profit, it pays for itself just by sitting there. The audience is captive and sun-focused (resorts, eyewear, travel brands), and no age controls are needed. Then stack a Generated layer — a QR code people scan for a hotel discount — to push it further past zero. Embedded base, Generated on top. Free pet food? You're advertising through the pet to a loyal, emotional owner; the packaging is roomy (Embedded), frequency is daily, and brands range from other pet products to insurance to cars. Free umbrellas at a train station on a rainy day? The umbrella is a walking billboard (Embedded), and the dwell time is the wait for the train — a perfect spot for a Generated layer. Do that — actually run the five questions — for whatever product you're dreaming about, and you'll feel it click. The only real limit isn't the product. It's how creatively you stack the layers. That's the whole method. Next, let's look at the surface all of this lives on — because your packaging isn't packaging anymore. It's an interface.

## Chapter 8: The Packaging Is the New Interface

Stop thinking of a water bottle, a food wrapper, or a delivery box as packaging. That's outdated. Your packaging is now an interface — and once you see it that way, you can't unsee it. Think about what a screen actually is. A phone's touchscreen, a TV's remote — they're just surfaces that connect you to something and let something happen. Your product's label, QR code, and design do exactly that now. You can show a physical ad, link to anything on the internet with a QR code or an NFC chip, launch an augmented-reality experience over the product, and collect first-party data about who engaged. Your product becomes a direct connection point between the real world and the internet — a touchscreen anyone can hold in their hand, and this one you can drink from. Once you think that way, everything changes — the artwork, the calls to action, the AR layer, all of it. So, before we get to what you put on the interface, let's look at what the interface makes possible. These are the ideas that turn a label into a media channel.

### One unit, many doorways

Depending on the size and shape of the package — a water bottle, a cookie wrapper, a bag of chips — you can carve out three or more clean, attractive ad slots on a single unit. So instead of selling one company a whole 24-pack, you can divide that pack among dozens of advertisers. And here's the ladder that matters: at the base level, this works with nothing but ink or a sticker. A broke beginner can start today. But add a little tech and each of those tiny slots becomes a doorway — to individualized content, an AI experience, mixed reality. The slot stops being a printed rectangle and becomes a portal. (In the language of Chapter 5: the ink is your Embedded base; the QR code and AR are Generated layers stacked on top.) You're not just selling ad space. You're selling access to the internet, through something a person is holding in their hand.

### Advertising, finally open to everyone

This next part is one of the most important ideas in the book, because it does to advertising what the rest of the model does to essentials: it breaks open a closed system. For a century, traditional advertising methods has been pay-to-play — gated behind four-figure minimums that lock out everyone but established brands. In a high-cost city like Amsterdam, a traditional campaign might require a €1,000+ minimum just to get on the table. But if you can sell one-sixth of a single bottle across a run of cases for, say, €25 to €50, you've just opened the door to the people who were never allowed in: micro-brands, startups, the local café, the indie musician, the side-hustler, the local shop down the street. (Those numbers are an illustration, not a price list — but the point holds: the entry cost collapses.) That's not a pricing tactic. It's access. The same way free water challenges who gets to drink, micro-slots challenge who gets to advertise.

### A pack printed for one household

When the system is built right, you don't send out one branded bottle — you send out packs, where every unit inside can carry different ads. One person opens a 12-pack of water and sees 12, 24, even 60+ different ads chosen for them — their household, their interests, their neighborhood, their past purchases. You can build a pack that's personalized to the home, a full takeover for a single advertiser, or a mix of brands across the available slots. In the near future, most of these packs will be assembled on demand — by smart vending machines, decentralized fulfillment hubs, and printing trucks that print each bottle or snack in real time, for the person about to receive it. That's not fantasy; it's already being prototyped.

### When packs become marketplaces

Now extend that. Once every slot is individually addressable, a 12-pack stops being a physical object and becomes inventory. Each slot can be bought, bid on, and optimized for performance — exactly like digital ad space today. Packs become marketplaces. Even a new kind of app store, where physical slots are traded like the most valuable real estate in advertising. Imagine a 12-pack of water with 12 brands, 12 links, and 12 chances to connect — each one priced by demand, each one measurable. That's where this goes.

### How the surface pays for the host

One mechanic ties all of this together and answers the question every business asks: Why would I bother? Because the surface can pay for itself, and then some. A hotel, an airline, or an event doesn't have to choose between branding its own water and selling the space. It can do both. Sell 75% of the packaging to noncompeting brands, and those partners can cover most, all, or even double the cost of producing the water. The host keeps the other 25% to promote its own services — and its bottled-water expense doesn't just shrink, it disappears, while it passively donates to a cause and gains PR. Reduced overhead, built-in exposure, and a smarter way to engage guests, all at once. That self-funding split is what makes adoption easy.

### The two branches: advertising vs. communication

Here's the framework that organizes everything you can put on the interface. The medium has two branches, and they are not the same thing: Advertising is when a third party pays to put a message in someone's hand. The bottle is media you sell. The goal is reach, response, and revenue — getting a stranger to act. Communication is when an organization uses the surface to speak to its own people — students, employees, residents, patients, members. The message isn't selling anything; its job is to inform, align, or help. But be clear about the business underneath, because it's easy to misread: the surface is still sold. A school district that orders labeled water is a paying customer, just like an advertiser — it's simply buying the surface for its own message instead of renting it out to a brand. (The one exception is the organization that runs its own product — a school printing on its own milk cartons owns the channel outright, with no operator in the middle.) Different payer, different purpose. Selling to a stranger versus speaking to your own. Keep that distinction in your head, because the best move is completely different depending on which one you're doing. Let's take each branch in turn.

### Branch 1: Advertising

The principle: A free product in someone's hand is the least-skippable, most useful ad medium on earth — so the right message on the right product can drive real-world action. Here's how that plays out across different advertisers. Hotels already give away free water — but most stop at slapping a logo on it. Instead, that bottle can promote tours, spa bookings, happy hour, or a return reservation. And by selling half or two-thirds of the space to non-compete brands, a smart hotel offsets the cost entirely. Car dealerships do the same thing badly — free water, no real ad. Done right, each bottle is a mini salesperson: a test-drive offer, a trade-in deal, an AR walkaround, a giveaway. Local service providers — real estate agents, insurance agents, lawyers, accountants — win with the simplest version: a photo, contact info, a testimonial. Agents have sold half-million-dollar homes off a bottle of free water. Banks should hand every client a free, ad-supported bottle to drive leads: refera-friend offers, a new credit card, a mortgage special, "get $100 when you sign up" — right there on the bottle. Luxury retail can use free water, coffee, or wine to keep shoppers in-store longer, with the season's best deals on the bottle. Even if the customer leaves, they carry the ad with them — for an hour, or for years if it rolls under the car seat. Pair it with retargeting ads to anyone who scans the QR code, and the bottle becomes the top of a funnel. Waiting rooms — doctors, dentists, mechanics — are easy. Nudge prices up slightly to cover the cost of handing out free water; the customer is grateful for the drink, and it markets every other service the business offers while they wait. Conferences are full of free junk that explains nothing — I've collected drawers of branded sunglasses and bottle openers that never told me what the company did. A free bottle with the company's best offer, a QR code to anywhere online, and an AR experience worth sharing beats all of it. (Watch for venues that charge booths extra, since many already sell overpriced water.) Street-traffic diversion is where this gets almost magical, because a message in someone's hand can physically change where they walk. Picture the Las Vegas Strip: Someone offers you a free, ice-cold drink on your way to the MGM Grand. Most people take it — and the label shows an offer from the Venetian: $25 in free play, 50% off the buffet, free entry to the nightclubs. A good share of those people change course on the spot. About 50,000 people walk the Strip daily; if 75% took a free drink and even 10% acted, that's over a hundred thousand people redirected to one hotel in a month. A bottle rerouted them. (Illustrative math — but the principle, that a physical offer in-hand can redirect foot traffic, is very real.) Stadiums can replace bobblehead night with a collectible bottle — a QR code to redeem merch and join a fan club, bottles handed out on the way in to hype next week's game and on the way out to help fans sober up. Retail stores can give a free water at checkout with the store's top offers printed on it: Shoppers stay and browse longer, and a mini billboard walks out the door with every customer and keeps selling after they leave. Music festivals can fold unlimited free water into the ticket price, with sponsors covering two-thirds of the space, the headliner on the front, and a QR code to their songs, merch, and tour dates. The festival gets paid, keeps everyone hydrated and safer (fewer liability problems), and a big multi-day event can donate hundreds of thousands to charity — next-level PR. Many fans keep the bottle as a collectible, and a scan months later can still serve a fresh offer. Political campaigns may be the most striking of all. In 2024, USA candidates spent a combined $10.5 billion on advertising — enough to hand every American a free 24-pack and donate over a billion dollars to charity. Imagine a candidate who spent their ad budget that way, then said: "Instead of using advertising to annoy and interrupt you, we delivered free essentials that donated millions to charity and helped save lives — before I was even elected. Imagine what we'll do next." Portable, shareable, built to go viral in the real world — and genuinely helpful. (The $10.5B figure is real; the rest is what's possible with it.)

### Branch 2: Communication

Now the other branch and notice how the whole posture changes. In advertising, you're selling the surface to promote a third-party brand. In communication, the surface is being used to distribute important information that goes beyond selling products — students, employees, residents, patients, members. There's no thirdparty advertiser in the deal — but don't mistake that for no revenue. The school, the employer, the city is your customer; what they're buying is delivery of their own message, attached to a gift. The payoff is different: a message handed to someone attached to a gift gets received, in a way no email, poster, or newsletter ever is. Here's how that plays out. Schools may be the purest case. A school hands out water or milk every single day — and every carton rides home in a backpack and lands on the kitchen table, which is the one place a school newsletter never reaches. Print the parent-teacher conference dates on it. The vaccination deadline. A QR code to the lunch menu, the tutoring program, the bus-route change. The school isn't selling anything. It's finally reaching the parent. Employers are sitting on the same channel and don't know it. Every office already gives away free coffee and water; almost none of them put a message on it. Hacker prevention, open-enrollment deadlines, the safety reminder that actually matters this month, the employee-of-the-month's face, the all-hands date — on the cup in everyone's hand instead of in the email nobody opens. Internal comms teams spend real budgets trying to get read. The cup gets read. Cities and public agencies have the highest-stakes version. During a heat wave, free cold water handed out downtown with heat-safety instructions and coolingcenter addresses printed on the label reaches exactly the people at risk — including people who will never visit a city website. A census reminder, a new transit line, the number for social services: a municipality that gives away water isn't just hydrating residents. It's operating a communication channel to the hardest-to-reach people it serves. Public health does this at the level of a single life. A bottle from a clinic with the flu-shot schedule on it is useful. A bottle with a mental-health hotline on it, sitting on someone's desk for a week, might be the difference nobody ever gets to measure. A poster on a wall waits to be noticed. A bottle in a hand has already been accepted. And disaster response is the branch's clearest proof, because it's already halfway built. Bottled water is the first thing distributed after every hurricane, flood, and earthquake — and today it goes out with blank or branded labels, a wasted channel at the exact moment information is scarcest. Print the shelter locations. The boil-water advisory. The QR code for aid registration, the missing-persons hotline. The water is already the infrastructure; the label should be the emergency broadcast. Of everything in this chapter, this is the version I most hope someone builds before the next storm makes the case for them. The same logic runs through churches, universities, nonprofits, and teams speaking to their own congregations, students, donors, and fans — event invites, volunteer sign-ups, a transparency report showing members exactly where their giving went. And yes, the line between the branches blurs at the edges: a stadium promoting next week's game on the bottle is "communication" shading back into advertising, and that's fine. The branches aren't a wall; they're two jobs the same surface can do. Remember the host split from earlier in this chapter — sell 75% of the surface to advertisers, keep 25% for your own voice. Most real-world bottles will carry both branches at once, and the operator who understands which job each slot is doing will always out-earn and out-communicate the one who doesn't. One more reason this branch matters, it's the on-ramp. A school district, a city, or a hospital that starts using free products as a communication channel becomes your distributor, your case study, and — once they see the engagement — very often your next advertiser conversation. The communication branch doesn't compete with the advertising branch. It recruits for it. So that's the interface, whole: one surface, two branches. Advertising monetizes it — a stranger pays to be in someone's hand. Communication mobilizes it — an organization or business finally reaches its own people. Same bottle, same label, same QR code; the only thing that changes is whose message rides on it and why. But every bit of it — both branches, every example in this chapter — depends on one thing we've been quietly assuming: that the surface itself is worth holding, worth photographing, and worth scanning. That's a craft of its own, and it starts with the most important hire you'll make. Next, let's design the surface and make the scan actually work.

## Chapter 9: The Surface and the Scan

One of the most important people on your team will be your graphic designer, and it's worth understanding why before you hire one. Your product is the ad. If it doesn't look great, none of the rest works — no advertiser pays a premium for ugly, and nobody stops scrolling for a bottle that looks like it came from a clearance bin. But here's the idea this whole chapter hangs on, and most designers won't grasp it at first: you are not really designing a bottle. You're designing the photo of a bottle. Think about how people will encounter your product. A few will hold one in their hand. But far more will meet it as an image on a screen — in your content, a friend's story, a stranger's post, scrolling past at arm's length on a phone. That bottle is going to live most of its life as a thumbnail. Once you accept that, every design decision gets easier, because they all answer the same question: will this look great, read clearly, and work as a small image on a phone? Hold that question in your head and most of what follows is common sense applied honestly. And it carries straight through to the QR code, because the code is the doorway that turns that photographed surface into an actual connection — which is the second half of this chapter. The hardest part, and the lesson no one warns a first timer about, is the curve. It sounds trivial — just wrap the design around the label — but a bottle is a curved, three-dimensional object, and curves distort flat artwork in ways that will blindside you. The hardest thing we ever had to print was certain cars. A car looks perfect on your flat screen, then it wraps around the bottle and the proportions bend and suddenly it looks wrong in a way you can't quite name. The same thing happens to faces, to straight lines, to logos, and to text — anything the eye expects to be rigid and correct is exactly what the curve betrays. This is what separates people who've printed bottles from people who've only designed flat labels, and the only protection is to never trust the screen. Print a real test label, wrap it around an actual bottle, and look at it in the real world. Use 3D software to preview how the wrap will behave before you commit. And get your client to sign off on that physical test, not the flat file, before anything goes to print. Skipping this is how you end up paying for a full run of bottles with a distorted logo on them. Once you've made peace with the curve, use the whole object. Every round bottle has three main frames that show when someone holds or photographs it, and most people waste two of them by repeating the same image or message. Don't. Even with only one advertiser, design three versions of the message across the three frames. It makes the bottle feel like a little collection rather than a static ad, it nudges people to rotate it to see each side, and — the part that matters for your business — it means every photo someone takes captures something different, which multiplies the impressions a single bottle generates. More unique faces, seen more times, is literally more value you can charge an advertiser for. It costs a bit more design work and it's one of the easiest ways to make a bottle worth more. The rest of looking premium is mostly refusing to look cheap. Don't use bad photos — a low-res or cheesy stock image makes the whole product feel disposable, while a crisp, well-composed one makes it feel like it belongs in a commercial. And don't overcrowd it. This isn't a newspaper or a brochure; it's a billboard, and billboards work because they say one thing boldly and land it in the three seconds you have while driving past. A strong logo, one powerful visual, a short tagline or call to action — that's the whole recipe. The instinct to fill every empty millimeter is the one to fight, because empty space is what makes the important thing readable at thumbnail size. While you're at it, give people a reason to act. Unless you're a luxury brand or the ad is genuinely funny or moving, a good offer does the heavy lifting — "scan for a free gift," "20% off your next ride," "watch this to win" — because a concrete incentive creates the little spark of curiosity that turns a glance into an engagement. A quick word on what you're printing on, since people always ask about water boxes. They're tempting: a flat carton reads more like a magazine page than a curved label, and if all four sides are equal you can fit something like twelve advertisements on a single 500ml box — an advertiser's dream. The catch is that the supply chain for boxes is long and fragile, and the printing machines cost ten to a hundred times what a bottle-label printer costs, so a single hiccup can halt everything. We started with boxes and eventually stopped, at least for now. The honest advice is the same as it is for painted glass and for Generated Value: incredible real estate, wrong place to start. Unless you own the factory or you're moving serious volume, begin with something simpler and come back to boxes when the economics are on your side. Now the doorway. A QR code is how that photographed surface becomes a living connection — scan it and you're taken to a website, a video, an app, a payment page, a game, anything online. When you're handing out a free product, that scan is where a lot of the real value happens, so it's worth getting right. The most important thing to understand is the difference between a static and a dynamic code, because it's a security issue, not a design one. A static code is locked forever — once it's printed, the link can never change. A dynamic code can be edited later, even after it's in people's hands. That flexibility is powerful, but it's also a danger, and it leads to the single most important rule in this chapter: if an advertiser sends you their own QR code, do not use it. Because if they control the code, they can change where it points after it's printed on millions of bottles — to spam, to malware, to something inappropriate sitting in a child's hand. You cannot guarantee an audience's safety with a code you don't control. Always generate the codes yourself, on a platform you trust, so you control the destination while the advertiser still gets the results. As your volume grows, you may even want to build your own QR generator — partly to save money at scale, and partly so that only your company holds the scan data, which matters more than you'd think and which we'll come back to. Then there's the simple matter of making the code work, which comes back to the screen. QR codes aren't meant to be cute; they're meant to scan, and that means resisting the urge to shrink them for the sake of a clean layout. Somewhere around half an inch square is the sweet spot, and much above an inch is overkill unless it's going on a billboard or a TV. Too small and older phones can't read it; too big and it overpowers the packaging. Put one on each of the three faces, centered on its panel. And test it the way people will use it—take a photo of the finished bottle and try to scan the code straight off the picture on your screen. If it scans from the photo, you're safe. If it doesn't, it's useless, because most people will meet your code in an image, not in their hands. The same goes for any website or phone number on the bottle: if it's too small to read or tap from a photo, it may as well not be there. Once the code works, make sure what's behind it earns the scan — a discount, a freebie, something useful or fun—because a good scan becomes a good experience, and a good experience is what makes someone show a friend and scan the next one. There's also a quieter piece of craft here that's really a negotiation, and it's worth more money than it looks. When you lay out your label, give your own brand and your manufacturer the smallest footprint you reasonably can, because every millimeter the two of you occupy is a slot you can't sell to a paying advertiser. That doesn't mean stripping off the required manufacturing and nutrition information—that isn't legal and you should never do it—but you can shrink those fonts and, where the law allows, tuck the nutrition facts behind a QR code to free up the surface. Be ready for this to be a slightly touchy conversation with your manufacturing partner, since they've probably always splashed their branding across the label and shrinking it shrinks their visibility too. It's a small fight worth having, politely, because on a product whose surface is your revenue, the space you reclaim is the space you get paid for. Here's where the scan becomes more than a link. Every time someone scans, you have a chance to understand your audience — and that understanding is what lets you improve your campaigns, prove results to advertisers, and build a real relationship with the people holding your product. But how you do this matters enormously, because there's a right way and a creepy way, and the whole spirit of this book lives on the right side of that line. Start with what you can collect immediately and anonymously. Using free or lowcost tracking platforms, every code can tell you total scans, the time and date, the device type, and a general city-level location. In most countries that's completely legal precisely because none of it is personal — just don't store anything sensitive without consent, and you're on solid ground. That alone is enough to know which products, locations, and designs are working. You can go a step further and collect names, emails, and phone numbers — but only through a genuine, transparent exchange, never by stealth. The honest way is simple: build a landing page behind the code and let people opt in for something actually worth it — a giveaway, a reward, early access — asking only for what you need and saving it to a spreadsheet or CRM. Always show people exactly what you're collecting and why, and always get their permission. This isn't only about staying legal, though it is the law in many places and you should check the rules where you and your users live. It's about staying on the right side of the User Test from Chapter 2: the person should feel they came out ahead for having scanned, not surveilled for it. That distinction is the entire difference between this model and the ad-tech world that harvests people quietly — and it's worth protecting, because it's the thing that makes your data trustworthy. When it's done that way, the payoff is real for everyone. With opt-in contact information, an advertiser can follow up with an offer, build a custom audience on Facebook or Google, retarget across the web, and finally measure return down to a single product or campaign. Picture it: someone takes a free bottle of water, enters their email for a giveaway, and a week later sees that advertiser's product again on YouTube, Instagram, and Google. The physical scan became a lasting digital connection — and because the person chose it, in exchange for something they wanted, it's a connection that builds loyalty instead of resentment. That's the whole chapter in one line: design the surface for the screen, make the doorway work, and let the scan teach you something — honestly. Do those three things and you don't just have a good-looking bottle. You have a free product that's genuinely worth paying to advertise on, and the beginnings of something far more powerful. Because all that scan data, collected the right way, becomes one of the most valuable assets in this entire model — a thing we'll come back to and call Honest Data. Next, let's make the world see it — how to build content that spreads without spending a dollar on ads.

## Chapter 10: Make Movements, Not Ads

If you take one idea from this chapter, take this one, because every tactic that follows is really just a way of serving it: don't make ads — make movements. The biggest mistake I see founders make is treating their content like old-school advertising — "my water is the best," "look at our new label," "sponsor us today." Nobody cares. It's boring, it's self-promotional, and it's not shareable. What spreads is something else entirely: people watching other people receive something for free, and reacting honestly. You're not selling a product. You're building belief. Get that straight and social media becomes the single most powerful growth engine you have — and the best part is it can all start with 200 bottles and a phone.

So, let's talk about why it works, how to make content that spreads, the system to multiply it, the habits that compound it, the discipline that protects it, and what happens when it finally hits.

### Why this works

People love watching others get something for free. It makes them feel good, curious, inspired — it's the same psychology that earns MrBeast hundreds of millions of views, and it doesn't matter whether it's a free car or a free cookie. The reaction is the same. And there's a second thing that happens on camera, every single time we film. Most people, it turns out, already believe water should be free, and they immediately want to see the same logic applied to food and everything else. Others can't wrap their heads around it: "So how does this actually work? It's got to be a trick. What's the catch? Are you a communist?" — yes, that question again. And then the moment comes when they realize the product is free because of the ads, not charity, and you watch their mind crack open. That moment is the magic. It's the same realization you had the first time you heard about FreeWater. The videos that capture that moment the fastest, with the most entertaining or thought-provoking reactions, are the ones that travel. Which tells you what you're actually chasing. Not likes — likes are the least important number there is, pure vanity. You want people to watch the whole video, ideally twice, and then comment and share. That's belief, and belief is what spreads. So never cheat it. Don't buy followers, don't buy views. You're changing the world, and that only counts if the growth is real.

### How to make a video that spreads

The most important thing I can teach you about content is that the first one to three seconds decide everything. People scroll fast, and the competition is infinite — beautiful people doing wild things, a cat playing piano, someone whose pet tiger is best friends with their two dogs, a shocking headline. If you don't hook a viewer instantly, they're gone before you finish your intro. Once you've got them, the whole goal is to get them past 51% of the video — ideally to watch it twice — and to comment or share. That's the formula.

The way you earn that attention is by stacking interesting things into the shortest possible window. The more compelling talking points you can pack into a few seconds, the better the video performs. A rough order of operations: the product is free and donates to charity; the person on camera says or does something unexpected; you spark a debate or an emotional reaction; you ask a question people don't see coming; you slip in a subtle twist; and you end in a way that leaves them wanting more. If you can land five or more of those beats in under ten seconds, you're doing great. There's also a sneaky trick worth knowing: occasionally load the screen with just enough text that it takes about six seconds to read, then cut the video at five. People are forced to rewatch to catch the end, and replays are gold to the algorithm. But the real secret to content that spreads is that you're not the star — you're the guide. Let the people receiving your product tell the story. This isn't only because it's better content; it's protection. If you make the movement about you, you put the whole thing at risk on your bad day, your bad month, or the day you say something that gets you canceled. So hand the moment to the people in front of the camera. Ask something raw and real — "do you think food and water should be free?" or "how would your life change if you never had to pay for these again?" — and then shut up and film. (You've got a fuller bank of these questions back in the guerrilla-launch chapter; two or three good ones is plenty for any single video.) The best content feels unscripted because it is. Every piece should make someone feel something, question the way things are, and want to tell a friend. That's how belief spreads — and you don't need a studio or fancy gear to do it. You need real moments, human emotion, and a mission that matters.

### The system that multiplies it

Here's the workflow that turns one video into many. Do the creative work once, on a single platform — TikTok or YouTube Shorts — because that's your testing ground. Post your giveaway video there. Watch your own video twice in a row, then swipe out of the app and leave it alone for at least thirty minutes. Come back and reply to a comment or two, always aiming to draw more responses, then copy the video link and swipe out again. Now search Google for "download TikTok videos without watermark," grab the HD file from one of the top results, and repost that same video everywhere else that matters: LinkedIn (personal and business), Instagram (feed, Reels, and Stories), Facebook, YouTube Shorts, Threads, Snapchat, X, and whatever else is relevant in your country. One video becomes seven. Do that two or three times a day and you build real momentum — and real SEO for your website, as long as your domain matches your social handles. In the beginning, you need to explode onto the internet, so post a lot — at minimum one to three times a day. I personally did three to ten posts a day when I was getting FreeWater off the ground. It sounds like too much, and it is, but there's a reason for it: it takes roughly 200 videos to get your sea legs, to start intuitively understanding what resonates. The faster you get through those 200, the faster you learn. Treat your home platform like a laboratory — post a lot, then wait two to four weeks to see what actually worked. Then take only the best performers and give them a second life on Instagram, about one a week. My criteria for a "best" video: a high view count, a like-to-view ratio near 10%, and a lot of comments and shares. The real winners come close to hitting all three.

### The habits that compound it

A few habits separate people who grow from people who burn out. The first is counterintuitive: do not use social media for entertainment on the same phone you use for work — or at the very least, don't doom-scroll for an hour or two right after posting. Post your content, reply to your comments, then get off the app. If you want to scroll for fun, use a separate phone. I'm serious about this, because you are not just using these platforms, you're training them. Every video you watch and every comment you linger on teaches the algorithm what to feed you and what to make your content into. If you doom-scroll junk, it'll feed you junk and your own content quality will follow. What you want instead is to post and walk away, and let the notifications pile up — this person liked, this person commented, this person shared — fifty or a hundred times before you even look. Training the algorithm to chase you like that leads to far more views. The second habit is knowing when not to post. As much as I push posting often, there's one moment to pause: when a video is going viral, don't step on it by dropping more content right behind it. Let the algorithm run. I'll wait anywhere from one to seven days depending on how hot it is, and I only post next when I've got something as good or better — that's how you chain viral videos together, one feeding the next. And the third habit is to never let good content die. After a year or two you'll have a library of classics that almost always perform, and you should repost them every three to six months. If a platform flags the repost, just tweak the edit slightly from your raw footage. A video that worked is still brand-new to most people. My biggest hit reached 35 million views on its own — and counting all the reposts across platforms, that one video probably got close to 250 million total, precisely because I keep running it back. You'll also get some of your best ideas from the comments, if you're paying attention through the noise. Ignore the trolls, but stay alert for the gold. Someone once pointed out that I should put my business card on the back of the bottle to make sales easier, and the comment got thousands of likes — my number was already there in the manufacturing info, I just wasn't communicating it. That gave me the line I started using: "the water is the business card," and that one line led to millions of views. Another time people told me my editing made the videos feel less authentic. They were right, so I changed it, and engagement went up. You'll get those tips too — but only if you read the room.

### The discipline that protects it

Here's a piece of strategy that's also a survival rule: stay out of every fight except the one about why your product should be free. This flows directly from the mission. You want everyone to drink your free water and eat your free food — not just the people who share your politics, your religion, or your values. So, the moment you publicly pick a side on anything else, you've potentially lost half your city, half your country, maybe half the world. I've been on live streams where people grill me like I'm running for president: Who'd you vote for? Is one religion better than another? And even when I have an opinion, I answer the only way that keeps the movement open to everyone: "I just want everyone to have the freedoms they desire, as long as it doesn't harm others. I want to give everyone free access to groceries, even people I might disagree with. I believe free products can bring people together and help the environment. That's all I care about."

This isn't just about persuasion — it's about staying alive on the platform. I've watched accounts with millions of followers get suspended or permanently banned because the creator decided it was their duty to weigh in on Trump, or gay rights, or firearms, and the people who disagreed banded together and massreported the account until it got flagged. Don't hand anyone that weapon. And on the human side: going viral is addicting, and on your worst day, at your lowest moment, that's exactly when a troll's comment will sting the most. Do not respond then. That's when you say the thing you can't take back. Don't pick sides, don't get pulled into conflicts, and when you're not at your best, step away from the keyboard.

### Going live

Live streaming is its own skill, and a healthy one will pour hundreds or thousands of people onto your channel and especially onto your most recent post. I like to post a video about thirty minutes before I go live, answer a comment or two, then fully swipe out until I'm ready. When you start, show people something worth watching — moving parts, beautiful scenery, even a clean and professional glimpse of your manufacturing line, bottles being filled or labels applied. Just never show anything sloppy on a live stream; it works against you. And it doesn't even have to be about your product. This spring my wife and I were in Egypt, and I ran a daily live from the temples and the Pyramids — 250 to 800 people each time. I mostly just talked about what I was seeing, but every five minutes I gave a shameless thirty-second shout-out to the FreeWater mission and held up a bottle. The best example of live done right is a guy who keeps showing up on my feed — the crazy fruit guy. He sits behind a table piled with exotic fruit and refuses to taste any of it until enough people are watching, liking, and sharing. I've seen 5,000-plus people glued to his every move, some tossing him gifts, which are really cash, others sharing the stream. He's pulled in more than four million followers this way. But here's the genius part: he's not in the fruit business at He's a musician, and the link in his bio goes straight to his songs. His live streams might earn him hundreds or thousands of dollars while quietly driving people to his music. He never posts videos about his music — only short clips of himself eating fruit, inviting people to the live. That's the whole strategy, and it's brilliant.

### When it hits

Let me be completely clear about the payoff, because I've lived it. One good video can change everything. Our first TikTok to hit 50,000 views brought 10,000% more traffic to our website than the time I spent $15,000 on Google and YouTube ads. Ten thousand percent. A couple of years later, with a better website and far more traction, we hired a digital marketing professional for a second try and another $10,000 ad budget — and they sold nothing. Organic social media still beat them by a hundred miles. That's not an argument against ever advertising; it's proof of where your energy belongs in the beginning. So, when you finally land that breakout video, shake every tree. DM it to business owners, message distributors, email decision-makers, tag people on LinkedIn — and watch closely who comments and who follows you, because opportunities hide there. I learned this the hard way: MrBeast followed me on TikTok and I didn't notice for months. If I'd been paying attention I might have messaged one of the biggest influencers on earth the moment he showed up. Instead, I reached out months later, got no reply, and of course was so nervous my message had typos in it. Let the content open doors — then actually walk through them. And aim wide, not narrow: don't make content for advertisers, make content anyone can relate to and everyone can believe in. Use your content like a fishing pole, and fish in the Pacific Ocean, not a tiny pond. When people fall in love with the mission, the advertisers and distributors come knocking on their own. One last thing, and it matters more than any view count. Even when someone gives you permission to film, be ready for what happens if the video explodes — a moment that felt small and fun in person can mean something very different to the person in it once millions of people have seen it. Several times, weeks or months after a video took off, the person in it has asked us to take it down. Legally, with consent or the right local laws, we didn't have to. We always did anyway, because the long-term trust and integrity of your brand are worth more than a few million views. Lead with empathy, every time. And on the front end, know the rules: some of your best content gets filmed in the wild, sometimes candidly, sometimes emotionally — so make sure you're somewhere it's legal, and get permission before you post. Some of our biggest videos, the ones that reached hundreds of millions, were filmed with permission after the moment happened. Content isn't a side hustle you outsource and forget — and you shouldn't outsource it anyway, because this is a skill you need to own. For a free product company, social media is your oxygen: it builds your audience, brings in your advertisers and your donations, and makes your mission visible to the world. This is where your brand lives. So, respect the game and play it well — and remember it's okay to take a day or two off when you're burnt out or just not on your A-game. The mission will still be there tomorrow.

## Chapter 11: Finding a Manufacturing Partner Without Losing Your Mind

Once you've proven people want your free or negatively priced product, it's time to level up. That means real manufacturing — real partners, real orders, real money. And, unfortunately, real headaches. This is one of the hardest parts of the whole journey, so let me walk you through it the way I wish someone had walked me through it, scars and all. Let me start with the truth, because it's meant to prepare you, not scare you: most manufacturers will overpromise and underdeliver. I don't just mean on price. They'll lie about their minimum order quantity, their production quality, their delivery timelines, even their basic capabilities. In the USA alone, six out of seven manufacturers failed us — I'm not being dramatic, they simply could not deliver what they swore they could. We are still waiting on a shipment of 15,000 bottles that was supposed to arrive in April of 2022. One of the seven, though, turned out to be one of the best business partners I've ever had. But that's one out of seven. Sit with that number, because it shapes everything that follows. Here's the mindset that number should give you, and it's the opposite of what most beginners walk in with. A great manufacturer is rarer than a great advertiser.

Advertisers are everywhere; a supplier who actually delivers, on time, at consistent quality, is hard to find and worth holding onto. So your job in this chapter is not to negotiate someone down or throw your weight around. Your job is to make a good manufacturer trust you. You're the one auditioning. Once you internalize that — that you need them more than they need you, at least at the start — every move in this chapter makes sense. Finding candidates is the easy part now. Ask ChatGPT or another AI to build you a list of potential manufacturers in your target region, then do the thing most people avoid: pick up the phone and start calling. The list gets you names; the calls get you the truth. And the calls are where you do your first and cheapest round of filtering, before you've spent a dollar. A few things to know going in. Don't open with a long save-the-world pitch — they don't know you, and a founder gushing about changing the world sounds like a flaky one who won't pay. Keep it simple: you want to produce branded water bottles. You don't even have to tell them the water is free. All they truly care about is whether you're genuine and whether you'll pay on time, so sound like a reliable customer, not a dreamer. Mention the donation only if you're genuinely committed to doing it — it can help, but never promise what you won't follow through on. On that first call, tell them plainly that every order you produce will have different artwork, and watch how they react, because it's a great honesty test. Some will tell you there's a "printing plate fee" every time the artwork changes. Respond politely that you'd heard modern digital printers don't use plates anymore, and see what they say. How they handle that one question tells you a lot — whether they're behind the times, or whether they're hoping you don't know better. Show them the label specs you want and ask if they'll do it; you'll find nearly every producer has their own way of doing things, and you're learning whether they bend to your needs or only their own. When it comes to price, resist the instinct to haggle. I know that feels backwards, but remember who's auditioning. Instead of grinding them down, tell them the truth: your goal is to prove yourself a reliable partner, and once you've demonstrated your value and started bringing consistent business, you'd appreciate revisiting the pricing conversation. Get their word on that and move on. Trust first, discounts later. Now the most important part of this entire chapter. Read it twice, because people who read the first edition skimmed past it and it cost them dearly.

### The Three-Batch Rule

Let me tell you what happens when you skip this, because it happened to me and other entrepreneurs who read the first version of this book and decided not to test their supplier three times. They got excited. They found an advertiser. That advertiser paid them real money to put their brand on a run of bottles. They placed the order with a manufacturer who swore up and down it would arrive on time and look great — and then it didn't. It came late, or it came wrong, or it didn't come at all. And now they were on the phone with a paying customer, apologizing, issuing a refund, watching their reputation crater before they'd even built one. In this model your reputation is your business, and an advertiser who got burned doesn't come back, and doesn't keep quiet about it. More than one reader came back to me with that exact story. It is the most avoidable disaster in this entire book, and here is how you avoid it. Before you trust any manufacturer with anything that matters, you order three separate minimum-quantity test batches. Three. Then you judge them on all three before you let them anywhere near a paying advertiser's deadline. I know exactly why people skip this. You're in a hurry. You're excited. And maybe money's tight, and three test batches feels like a luxury you can't afford. So, you tell yourself one batch is enough, or none is, and it'll probably be fine. I want to flip that math for you, because the logic is exactly backwards: testing isn't the expensive option — skipping the test is. Three small batches cost you a few hundred to a few thousand dollars. Skipping them costs you a paying advertiser's refund, your reputation at its most fragile moment, and possibly the whole business. You cannot afford not to test. So why three, and not one? Because one successful batch proves nothing. Anyone can get lucky once. You are not testing whether a manufacturer can deliver a good batch — you're testing whether they do it reliably, every time, because reliability is the only thing that matters once someone has paid you. Across those three batches, watch two things above all. The first is the timeline: did each batch actually arrive on the date they promised? Late is the killer in this business, more than almost anything else, because late is what makes you break a promise to someone who paid you. The second is consistency: is the print clean and undistorted on the curve, is the water or product itself genuinely good, and did the quantity and specs match what you ordered — not just once, but all three times? If they pass all three, wonderful. You've found something rare, and you can start lining up your first advertisers with confidence. If they fail even one, walk away. Immediately. I don't care how far along you are or how much you like them — at this stage, one bad batch becomes a bad review you cannot survive, so the discipline to leave the moment they stumble is what protects everything you're building. And here's the sequencing rule that would have saved every person who got burned, so make it law: never sell an advertiser a deadline you haven't already proven your manufacturer can hit. The people who got hurt took the money first and hoped the manufacturer would come through. Do it the other way. Validate delivery across your three batches, then go sell deadlines you know you can keep. Get that order right and the whole nightmare simply never happens to you.

### Don't stress — you'll use every single bottle

Now, if the thought of placing a real order is making you nervous, let me put that fear in its place, because it's the fear that paralyzes people at exactly the wrong moment. Even if you're staring at 1,800 bottles wondering what you've done, relax: you're going to turn them into a thousand-plus pieces of content. They're not inventory you're stuck with. They're marketing fuel. Every bottle is a conversation, a TikTok, a testimonial, a step toward your next advertiser. Don't let the fear freeze you — let it move you forward with caution and confidence.

### You have more options than they admit

Where you're based matters, a lot, and the first "no" you hear is rarely the real answer. In India, Pakistan, parts of Africa, Asia, and some of Latin America, you can often find manufacturers willing to start as low as 250 bottles — especially if they see long-term potential in you or don't run on a pallet-based system. In the USA, Germany, Australia, and much of Western Europe, manufacturers may claim their minimum is a full truckload, or even ten. Don't take that at face value. Dig deeper, make the right connections, explain your goals clearly, and you can often find someone willing to start with a single pallet — typically 1,000 to 1,800 bottles depending on size. It's not easy, but it's far more possible than it used to be.

When the bottle path is closed to you — build a machine instead But let me be honest about something, because forcing the wrong model on the wrong situation is how good people fail. Sometimes the bottle path is simply blocked, and there are two different walls that block it. The first is the testing wall: you can find a small-batch supplier, but you genuinely cannot afford to test them three times before betting an advertiser's money on them. The second is the access wall: the only manufacturers within your reach won't deal in small numbers at all — they want 20,000 bottles up front, tens of thousands of dollars before you've sold a single ad. The door is locked unless you're already big or already funded. Both walls trace back to the same root cause: you don't own your factory. When you outsource, you live at the mercy of other people's minimums, other people's deadlines, other people's quality — and if the only suppliers you can reach won't do a small run, you're stuck before you begin. If that's you, hear me clearly: that is not a sign to give up. It's a sign to switch models. Remember Hope Hydration from the first chapter — the company putting free, purified water into units with ad screens? That's the other door, and it doesn't have either wall. Instead of bottles, you build a digital water dispenser: a unit that purifies ordinary tap water and runs digital ads on its screens, funded entirely by those screens. There's no manufacturer to convince, because the water is just filtered tap water. There's no minimum order to clear, because you're building one machine, not buying twenty thousand bottles. There's no shipment that fails to arrive in April, because there's no per-order supply chain at all. You build the unit, you place it somewhere with foot traffic, and you run a website with an advertiser back-end where brands upload their own digital content straight to the screens. The gatekeeper is gone. Notice how different the shape of the difficulty is. The bottle model is cheap per unit but carries a fragile, recurring risk that lives outside your control with a supplier you have to re-trust on every order. The dispenser flips that: the hard part is front-loaded and one-time. You have to get the machine right once, and then the worst part is behind you. For a founder who's locked out of factories or can't afford repeated testing, that's not a consolation prize — it's often a bettershaped problem, and it happens to be the first model in this book where a solo founder actually owns their means of production. When you own the machine, nobody can gatekeep you. I won't pretend it's a free lunch. Owning the machine means you now carry the build itself, the water-purification safety and certification, the maintenance, the placement permissions, the power, and the realities of vandalism — and Hope Hydration raised $26 million precisely because hardware at scale isn't cheap either. So, it's a real trade: you swap a gatekeeper you can't get past for a build you control. But for the right person in the right situation, that's exactly the trade worth making.

### A note to the manufacturers reading this

If you're an established manufacturer who picked up this book — and some of you have — this chapter probably read like an indictment. Let me flip it into an opportunity, because you're sitting on the rarest, hardest-to-get assets in this entire model. You already have the production, the supply chain, and the volumes that founders are desperate to access. You can do something they can't: add a high-margin, ad-funded product line to capacity you've already paid for — or become the reliable supplier that free-product founders are searching for and almost never finding. Being the rare manufacturer who actually delivers isn't a burden in this new economy. It's one of the biggest opportunities in it.

Find the rare good one, earn their trust before you ask for anything, test them three times before you bet a paying customer on them — or, if the factory door is closed to you, build a machine nobody can gatekeep. Get this part right and you've got a foundation the rest of the business can stand on. Next, let's talk about logistics — getting your product from the factory to the people.

## Chapter 12: D2C vs. B2B — How to Distribute Your Free Product

You've got your product. It's free, or better than free. Now comes the next big question, and it's one most founders underestimate: how do you actually get it into people's hands? Getting the product made is only half the job. Getting it distributed is the other half, and it's just as important — a brilliant free product sitting on a pallet helps no one. There are two main paths, and the fastest way to understand them is this: one is the spark, and one is the firewood.

Direct-to-consumer, D2C, is the spark. It's you or your team handling distribution and the public-facing experience — street teams with coolers on corners, pop-up stands, kiosks, shops, home delivery, neighborhood drops. It's the flashy stuff you see in the videos. Business-to-business, B2B, is the firewood. You ship bulk orders to other companies, nonprofits, or event organizers, and they hand the product out on their end. It's quiet, it's unglamorous, and it's where the real volume lives. You'll almost certainly build both. The whole point of this chapter is understanding what each one is for. Let me start with the one nobody posts about.

### B2B: the quiet backbone

Our social media is full of energetic street giveaways — Austin, New York, Philadelphia, San Antonio, Kenya, Egypt, Saudi Arabia, across Europe. But that's not the whole story, and honestly, it's not even most of it. Most of our business is B2B, and it is not exciting in the slightest. We ship an order straight from our manufacturer to a business, a nonprofit, or an event organizer who are the advertisers, and they distribute it themselves — at conferences, stores, schools, churches, events, wherever fits their audience. That's it. No viral videos, no street content. Just clean logistics. And that simplicity is precisely its strength. B2B is easy to scale, easy to fulfill, lower effort, higher volume, and deeply attractive to advertisers who just want hands-off impact without running a street team. If your goal is growth, margin, and operational efficiency — and eventually it will be — B2B becomes your foundation. It's the firewood that actually keeps the fire burning.

### D2C: the public face

D2C is the opposite kind of work. It's the street giveaways, the viral reactions, the targeted home deliveries, the neighborhood-by-neighborhood drops. It takes far more effort — logistics, team coordination, showing up in person — and it's slower. But the payoff is everything the firewood can't give you: you control the experience, you capture the content and the feedback, you build your brand directly with real people, and you collect the honest data that ends up powering everything else (more on that later, because it's one of the most valuable assets you'll build). D2C is harder and slower, but it's what people remember. It's the spark that gets the fire lit in the first place. In the early days you'll lean D2C, because you're testing the waters, making videos, building trust and buzz. As you grow, you'll discover that B2B is where the volume comes from. The spark gets attention; the firewood creates scale. Build both, and never confuse which job each is doing.

### Vending machines: the bridge between them

Vending machines are a clever hybrid that resolves the tension between the two. Technically they're D2C — a member of the public walks up and gets the product — but they function like B2B, because they can be stocked and run at scale without you standing there. They give you consistent distribution, built-in scarcity (one per person), valuable data, hyper-local targeting, and daily donations and impact, all running on their own. Think of vending machines as the bridge between your public face and your operational backbone — the thing that's somehow both spark and firewood at once.

### The secret that drives all of it: close advertisers first

Now the single most important operational insight in this chapter, and it's the one beginners get backwards. Your instinct, once you have product, will be to go find people to hand it out. That's the wrong first move. You don't start by finding distributors. You start by closing advertisers. Because the moment you've secured the ad spend, you suddenly know everything you need to know: how much product has to move, how often it needs to go out, and how much budget you have left over to incentivize a distributor if you even need to. The ad spend is the instruction manual for the entire operation. Lock it first, and every other decision gets clear and you stay in control. Try to do it the other way — line up distribution and then go hunting for advertisers — and you're guessing at volume, timing, and budget, which is how people flail. And here's the reassuring part that follows from doing it in the right order. Once the ad spend is locked, finding people to distribute is often the easiest part of the whole business. A solo volunteer, a passionate shop owner, a school, a local

NGO, a massive music festival — everyone loves giving away a free product, especially one that supports a cause, because it makes them look good, feel good, and draw positive attention. "Wait — I get to hand people something they actually want, and it donates to charity? I'm in." You'll hear some version of that more often than you'd believe. But you earn that easy moment by closing the advertiser first.

### The freight that actually matters

Distribution is one of the most overlooked and most expensive parts of this whole business, so let me give you the freight knowledge that actually protects you — the stuff that blows up deals, before the stuff that's just good hygiene. The first rule: make the advertiser cover freight, always. We never bake shipping into our prices. It's paid directly by the advertiser or added as a separate line item, for one simple reason — rates fluctuate constantly, and you are not running a trucking company. You're running a media company. Don't let unpredictable freight costs eat the margin of a media business. The second one is the single most useful thing in this section, because it's the trap that turns a happy advertiser into one who's out for blood. If a shipment is going to a conference or a major event, you must be crystal clear about delivery, because these venues assign brutally strict booth-delivery windows — a one-totwo-hour slot like "Wednesday between 10 a.m. and noon." Standard freight carriers will not hit that window. They'll miss it, the venue will reject the shipment, and everyone loses — most of all your reputation. So for any event, the advertiser must either pay for a dedicated truck or arrange to pick the order up themselves. There's no middle option, and saying so plainly up front saves the relationship. That leads to the dedicated-truck-versus-LTL decision, which is really just a question of how tight your deadline is. Less-than-truckload shipping is cheaper but slower, with more handling and a higher risk of damage, which is fine when timing is loose. A dedicated truck costs more but it's reliable and direct, which is what you need for events or any tight window. And whatever you choose, remember that fewer stops means fewer problems — don't get seduced by the cheapest quote if it means your pallets hop between five terminals, because every extra terminal is another chance for them to be crushed or lost.

Beyond those, the standard hygiene is quick: wrap your pallets tightly and label them clearly on all sides, use protective corners and shrink wrap even when it costs a little more, insure anything high-value, and track everything and get proof of delivery so you can confirm arrival. None of that is glamorous, but skipping it is how good product turns into damaged product. And let me give you the most expensive lesson I learned the hard way, the one I hinted at earlier in this book. If your shipment is heading into a snowstorm, you have to pay for a heated truck — because water freezes, and frozen product is ruined product. We didn't, once, and we lost the shipment to the cold. It was costly and it was embarrassing, and it's the kind of obvious-in-hindsight mistake that only stings once if someone warns you first. So consider yourself warned.

### Your logistics depend entirely on where you are

How you move product looks completely different depending on your country, and you should design around your reality rather than copy someone else's. In the US, Europe, and similar markets, freight is almost always palletized — warehouses, forklifts, loading docks, LTL or full-truckload carriers, strict schedules. In places like Uzbekistan, India, or Thailand, where low-cost labor is available, logistics are far more flexible and you can often skip the heavy infrastructure entirely. Don't assume the Western, palletized way is the "real" way — that manual, flexible approach can actually scale faster in those markets than a high-budget operation can in Germany or the UK. Match your distribution to where you actually are.

### How deep this goes: freight that pays you to ship

Here's an idea that shows just how far this model reaches, because it pushes even your costs to the left of zero. If it's ever feasible to own or lease your own delivery trucks or vans, treat those vehicles as exactly what your packaging already is: ad real estate. The surface of your truck is a moving billboard. Wrap it in ads, and if you've built the digital layer for your product, put it on the trucks too, so you're collecting the same data and can charge advertisers accordingly. In a city with heavy traffic or dense foot traffic, a well-designed truck covered in ads can reach tens of thousands of people a day — and depending on your local regulations, that exposure can generate enough ad revenue to cover your fuel, your maintenance, even your drivers' salaries. You can run static wraps, a QR code with a real-time call to action, or eventually screens and projectors for dynamic ads. Do it well and your freight stops being a cost and becomes revenue — your shipments literally start paying you to ship them. That's the number line from Chapter 2 applied to logistics: even the cost of moving your product can cross zero. As a bonus, every mile gives your advertisers exposure before the product is ever handed out.

### What can go wrong — and how to prevent it

Distribution isn't always clean. Sometimes people hoard the product, resell it for a profit, or hand it out irresponsibly. It happens, and it's manageable. We always use simple contractual agreements — and it's even better when the advertiser has lined everything up and taken responsibility for the whole chain. The contracts aren't meant to be scary, just clear: the product must be given away free, at the agreed frequency, never resold or misused, and any donation or charitable claim must be honored. That last point matters and ties back to the 10% Rule — the giving has to be real and verifiable, not a claim, so make sure your distribution actually delivers the impact you promised. But more important than any contract are your instincts. Don't work with someone just because they seem interested. Work with the people who get it — who believe in the mission, who care about the impact. Feel it out, meet them if you can, start with a small test, and scale only if it works. And don't overbuild on day one: start small, even delivering out of your own car, and expand your capabilities from there. Move your product as deliberately as you make it. Let D2C be your spark and B2B your firewood, close your advertisers before you chase your distributors, protect every shipment like your reputation depends on it — because it does — and remember that in this model, done right, even the trucks can pay for themselves. Next, let's meet the machine that bridges both worlds — the free vending machine.

## Chapter 13: The Future of Free Vending Machines

We didn't invent the vending machine. But we may have invented a better reason to use one. A free vending machine is exactly what it sounds like: you walk up, press a button, and get something for free. No payment, no tricks — a real product, fully paid for by advertising. It's one of the most powerful tools in this entire model, and if you couldn't clear the manufacturing wall in the last chapter, it's very likely your path, because a machine is the one piece of infrastructure a solo founder can actually own. But there's one question everyone asks before anything else, so let's answer it properly — because I promised you on page one that I would.

### "Won't someone just take everything?"

It's a fair concern, and the honest answer starts somewhere most people don't expect: it depends entirely on where you are. In a lot of well-organized, high-trust places — parts of the Gulf like Dubai and Saudi Arabia, much of Japan, and plenty of other orderly, low-crime regions — you need no security at all. You set the rule, "please take one," or whatever's permitted, and people simply follow it. Adding cameras and locks in a place like that isn't just unnecessary overhead; it can quietly insult the very people who would never dream of abusing it. So, the first thing to figure out isn't which security system to build. It's whether you need one at all. Read your environment first. In lower-trust environments, then you layer in controls appropriate to the place, and we designed our machines to make that easy. Some are guarded by a real person. Some require an app, a QR code, or an NFC tap to unlock a product. Some use cameras or simple logic to allow only one product per person per day. The amount of security you need is purely a function of where you operate — some places need none, some need a lot — and matching it to the environment is the whole skill.

But here's the deeper truth, and it surprised us: even where you could expect abuse, you usually see less of it than you'd think, because of what the product is. When people learn that the free item also donates to a cause, they tend to act with respect. Make something free and meaningful, and people don't rush to abuse it — they appreciate it. The giving doesn't just fund the product; it changes how people behave around it. Now let's look at how these machines grow up — from a simple giveaway into a full media, commerce, and data platform.

### The roadmap: one machine that grows up

Think of what follows not as six separate machines, but as one machine that gets smarter over time. With the right approach, the same basic hardware can climb this ladder rung by rung, by adding more sophisticated technology as you grow. You start with almost nothing and graduate, rung by rung. Two things improve as you climb, and they're worth watching as a pair. First, the machine gets better at putting the right ad in front of the right person — starting with a human doing it by eye, ending with real-time bidding that prints the perfect ad on your unit. Second, and quietly just as important, the machine gets better at understanding the person — collecting richer and more trustworthy information at every step. Targeting and data climb together, model by model, and both reach their peak at the top of the ladder. A quick map before we climb: Models 1 through 3 are the rungs of that single machine getting smarter, and you can start on Model 1 today. Model 4 is the odd one out — a different idea entirely, which I'll flag when we get there. Models 5 and 6 are the network and the frontier. Here's each one.

### Model 1: Human-Guarded — the on-ramp

This is the lowest-tech version, and it doesn't even require a machine. A cooler, a tent, table, and a person handing out free product — that's a Model 1 vending operation. It works beautifully at events, schools, concerts, and gatherings, and it's the natural starting point for the beginner, the cash-poor founder, and the reader who couldn't get past the factory minimums last chapter. It requires almost nothing: product, a person, and a spot.

But don't mistake "low-tech" for "simple-minded," because the person standing there is doing three jobs, not one. They prevent abuse, where the environment calls for it. They gather real human reactions and feedback — this is your richest content-and-learning stage, a live focus group on the sidewalk. And here's the part most people miss: if your cooler or machine holds more than one ad — different bottles carrying different messages — that person becomes a human targeting engine. They can read who's walking up and hand them the most fitting ad: the parent gets the bottle with the kids'-product ad, the construction worker gets the tool-brand ad, the tourist gets the local-attraction ad. A human reading the room and matching the right message to the right person is doing, by instinct, exactly what the high-tech models will later do with data. Model 1's guard is the prototype for the whole roadmap's intelligence. When demand at a spot proves steady, you replace the person with a lock — and you've reached Model 2.

### Model 2: Smart Unlock — remove the human

Take a real vending machine, often an existing one you've retrofitted, and strip out the cash and coins entirely. Instead, the user taps an app, scans a QR code, or taps an NFC tag to unlock their free product — some number of units per day, handled entirely by software. The obvious win is that you've removed the labor cost of a guard. The real win is subtler: to unlock the machine, the user opts in through your app, and that's where your data collection quietly begins. This is the pivotal step where the machine stops being a giveaway and becomes a platform, because now you have identity — you know who's using it. And the most important asset you've built here isn't the machine. It's the app. Hold onto that, because the app is the foundation for everything above this rung.

Model 3: Semi-Smart + Logic — the machine knows who you are Same hardware as Model 2, but now the machine recognizes the app user and acts on it. As someone approaches, the screen built into the machine can show them a targeted ad — just like social media does. Or, more cleverly: imagine the machine holds fifty-plus different bottles, each carrying a different printed ad.

When a user steps up, the machine dispenses the specific bottle whose ad best matches them. That last mechanic is more valuable than it sounds, because it solves a real problem — you never want the wrong ad reaching the wrong person. You'd never want a beer ad handed to someone in recovery, or a baby-product ad given to someone with no kids. Matching the right physical ad to the right human means every impression counts, and that's exactly what advertisers pay premiums for. This is the first big revenue jump on the ladder, because targeted impressions are worth multiples of untargeted ones, just as we covered back in the chapter on reach and audience.

### Model 4: Earned-Credit Redemption — the odd one out

Now the model that's different from all the others, so let me flag it clearly: every other model on this list is about your own free machine. Model 4 isn't. It's about making the paid world free — and it's the Generated-Value idea from Chapter 5, applied to vending. Here's the mechanic. You build an app that gives users credits — call them points, tokens, or rewards, whatever fits — in exchange for their time and attention: watching videos, creating content, answering surveys, completing challenges, time banking, and so on. Those credits hold a value in the user's local currency, say ten credits to a dollar, and the app lets them spend those credits like money. So, a user who's earned twenty credits walks up to a normal vending machine that sells a Pepsi for two dollars — a machine giving nothing away, charging full price — and "freely buys" it with their credits. The machine's owner gets paid in full; the user gets a free, ad-funded Pepsi with no ad printed on it anywhere. Their attention was the currency. And don't get hung up on the word "crypto" here — the credit is just a unit of earned value, and it can be a point, a reward balance, store credit, or, yes, a token, depending on how you build it. The expansive part is this: with the right architecture, that same earned-credit system isn't limited to your machines. It can work at any business that accepts digital payment — a supermarket, a gas station, an online checkout, anywhere Visa, Mastercard, and the like are accepted. That's the bridge from "free vending" to "free anything, anywhere money moves."

One honest caution, because this one has real-world edges: depending on how you build it — especially once those credits become redeemable as real currency across many businesses — you may be touching financial regulations like moneytransmission and identity rules, and the card networks' own terms. A simple closed-loop credit system and a full open-currency system are very different risk levels. So, build the vision, but get proper legal and compliance advice before you turn credits into something spendable everywhere. Don't improvise that part.

Model 5: Dynamic Ad Screens — the machine becomes a billboard network Now we scale from a machine into a network. Picture machines with electronic billboards on the front, and a website where advertisers buy screen time across the whole fleet — a single location or every machine at once, at a chosen date, time, and frequency, or shown only to the right person as they approach. This is the model the locked-out founder from the last chapter is really building toward, and it's the same idea behind Hope Hydration's screen-funded water stations. What makes this powerful is that you now have two revenue surfaces instead of one. The screen ads can fully fund products that can't carry a printed ad at all — the Cokes, Fijis, Oreos, and Snickers of the world, brands that own their own packaging and won't sell that surface, exactly the case we covered in Chapter 5. And if you distribute a broad range of free products, you will produce honest data too. Or you maximize revenue by stocking generic snacks and drinks that carry three layers of printed ads and run screen ads on top. The advertiser backend becomes a real product here: brands upload their own creative, choose locations, times, and targeting, and see their results — a programmatic billboard network in physical space.

### Model 6: Real-Time Customization — the frontier

This is the future, and I'll frame it honestly as exactly that: where this is heading and being developed, not what's humming on a corner near you tomorrow. In Model 6, the machine doesn't just dispense a product — it prints your personalized one before it drops. The packaging design, the QR code, and even the AR layer are all customized to you in real time, and advertisers bid to be the one who gets to place their message on your unit, exactly like Google Ads — except instead of paying for a click, they're paying to print on a real bottle delivered instantly to the right person. The machine knows who you are from data you've opted into, advertisers bid in real time, the highest relevant bidder wins, their label gets printed, and the product drops. It introduces a new kind of pricing — cost per print, CPP — alongside the cost-per-click you already know. There are up to five biddable layers on a single unit: the printed packaging, the QR code, the AR experience attached to the bottle, the digital ad on the machine's screen, and even AR that springs from the machine itself. And there's a premium twist — if someone chooses to pay for their water, they unlock the ability to design their own bottle across all three physical layers, add bonus features, and pick their own apps. In rare outlier cases, the stacked layers and premium options can push a single unit to tens, hundreds, even thousands of dollars in value. And the same print-on-demand capability isn't limited to vending — it applies to factories, to the printing trucks we discussed, and to the hyper-targeted 12-packs from the packaging chapter. Model 6 isn't just the future of vending; it's the future of the entire grocery store industry. How much could that be worth? Some of the most expensive online ads today — in finance, insurance, and legal — run forty to a hundred dollars or more per click. A machine like this has the genuine potential to rival or beat those rates in the right physical placement, because the ad appears on something that's free, essential, makes the person happy, funds a cause, and provides measurable feedback through scans and clicks. Emotion, necessity, and measurability together can make a single interaction worth more than any billboard, TV spot, or banner ad. That's the potential — run your own numbers for your own placement before you bank on it.

### What it costs to build

People always want a price, and the honest answer is that it depends on two things you can actually control. The first is how close you are to China, because China has by far the most vending-machine options and typically the most affordable ones — the nearer you are to that supply chain, the cheaper and more flexible is your sourcing. The second, and the bigger lever, is how handy you are: can you build it yourself or do you need to hire it out? We did a combination of both and it’s expensive because we are building things that haven’t been done before so mistakes are frequent. A founder who has to pay someone for every retrofit and every line of software will spend considerably more. And finally, what level of security and targeting are you planning to incorporate? So, before you ask, "what does a machine cost," ask "how much of this can I build myself, and how close am I to the source" — because those two answers set your real budget.

### What one machine can actually do — and then a fleet

Let me show you the impact, built up honestly one step at a time rather than in a single dizzying leap. Start with one machine in a high-traffic spot — say Times Square. Each machine holds around 350 bottles and, depending on crowd flow, might be emptied five to ten times a day. Using a midpoint of roughly 2,625 bottles a day, that's about 79,800 bottles a month and roughly 958,000 a year from a single machine. At a dime of donation per bottle — while still turning a profit through printed and screen ads — that one machine generates around $7,900 in donations a month, or about $95,800 a year. And it isn't the end of the value chain: if the system pays ten cents for every unit stocked, that same machine generates close to $95,800 a year for the person managing and restocking it. One machine: a meaningful donation, and a real job. Now scale to a handful — ten machines. The math is linear: roughly $958,000 a year in donations, and close to $958,000 a year supporting a small team of people keeping them filled. Already you've funded a serious cause and employed a crew, from ten machines. Now imagine many — a real fleet across a city, or a thousand machines nationally. You're approaching $100 million a year in donations to clean water or any cause you choose, alongside thousands of well-paying restocking jobs, all powered by free product nobody paid a cent for. These are illustrations based on a strong location, not guarantees — your real numbers depend on your placement and demand — but the climb is the point: even one machine does real good, and the fleet is just that same engine, multiplied.

### Operating one in the real world

Here's the reassuring part about actually running these: most of it is no different from running any vending machine. Placement matters — you want foot traffic and the right permissions for the spot. You'll deal with power, connectivity, and maintenance like any machine operator, and you'll want to think about the security of the machine itself, not just the product inside it. But there's one operational fact that is genuinely different, and you have to plan for it: how fast it empties. A normal vending machine sells slowly and gets restocked now and then. A free machine in a good location gets drained several times a day, because "free" drives a volume a paid machine never sees. So the unique operational challenge of this model is restocking velocity — keeping a high-demand machine full. And that "problem" is the best news in the chapter, because the constant restocking is exactly what creates the jobs. The thing that makes these machines demanding to run is the same thing that puts a paycheck in someone's pocket.

### The part you'll fully understand later

One more thing before we close, and I'm planting it now on purpose. Everything these machines collect as they climb the ladder — who showed up, what they chose, what they scanned, what they engaged with, and at the top of the ladder even what they were willing to pay for when a free version sat right beside it — adds up to something rare. Later in this book there's a concept called Honest Data, built on five tiers of genuinely trustworthy information, and a negatively priced vending machine with the right architecture is one of the only things on earth that can capture all five tiers at once. You don't need to understand that yet. Just know that as your machine grows from Model 1 to Model 6, it's quietly becoming one of the most valuable data instruments in this entire economy — and that Model 6's option to pay for a premium bottle when a free one is right there isn't only extra revenue. It's capturing the single most valuable signal there is. We'll come back to why.

### How big this gets

The total size of the free and negatively priced vending industry is hard to pin down, but it's easily heading into the tens of billions of dollars a year, because these machines earn from every angle at once: printed ads, QR codes, affiliate links, digital screen ads, real-time bidding, and trustworthy user data. Even with conservative adoption, the model can fund hundreds of millions — potentially billions — in charitable donations annually. The real power is in the scale. As more companies compete to place individualized ads and the technology gets smarter, these machines won't just replace the old ones. They'll redefine them — into global engines of profit and impact that hand out free goods, create well-paying jobs, and change the way the world thinks about advertising, consumption, and giving, all at the same time. You can start that with a cooler and a folding table this weekend. Next, let's talk about the people who pay for all of it: how to actually find advertisers and get them to say yes.

## Chapter 14: Pricing and Selling Your Ad Space

Most new founders choke the moment it's time to sell, and almost always for the same reason: deep down, they feel like they're asking for a favor. They get shy, or scripted, or pushy, because some part of them believes they're begging for money. So let me fix that belief before we touch a single price or script, because it's the thing that actually determines whether you succeed. You are not begging. You are offering a brand something genuinely more valuable than the ads they're already buying — a chance to increase their visibility, give to a cause, and get public credit for it, all wrapped in a product people are grateful to receive. Most brands are tired of pouring money into ads that get skipped in two seconds, sit next to garbage content, and make them look greedy. You're holding the opposite of that. Once you walk into the room knowing you're the one offering value, everything else in this chapter becomes execution. And here's the deeper truth that the rest of the chapter will earn: real confidence doesn't come from bravado. It comes from competence — knowing your price, knowing your buyer, and knowing your craft. Let's build that.

### Price from a number everyone already understands

If you've never sold advertising, pricing feels terrifying because it feels like a guess. So don't guess — borrow a price everyone already understands. There is nothing more familiar than a bottle of water. Here's the whole method: match the price of a similar bottled water in your local stores. If you're using a standard 500ml bottle, go see what that size costs from Nestlé, Dasani, Aquafina, or a local brand in your area. If it's the equivalent of fifty cents, start there. Now you can say to an advertiser, with a completely straight face: "For fifty cents per bottle, your brand goes directly into someone's hand, along with all the goodwill of giving something away for free." It's simple, understandable, and relatable — and most importantly, it means your price isn't something you invented and have to defend. It's anchored to a number the advertiser already accepts as normal. That anchor is what lets a nervous beginner name a price out loud without flinching.

### What you're actually selling

Before you sell it, you have to be crystal clear in your own mind about what "it" is — because if you think you're selling water, you'll undercharge forever. You're not selling water. Let me prove it with one fact: someone scanned a FreeWater QR code more than two years after they received the bottle. Two years. Try getting that from a banner ad that vanished in two seconds. That's what you're really selling — an ad that gets remembered instead of skipped. Specifically, you're selling a bundle no other medium can offer at once: a hightrust delivery, where your ad is physically handed to a real person with a smile, as a gift; built-in goodwill, because the product is free and makes the brand look generous; social content, because you're filming reactions and the sponsor becomes the hero of a shareable story; and real impact, because this ends up in someone's home, bag, or fridge, gets scanned, and starts conversations. Tie it back to the number line from Chapter 2: every other ad lives on the right side of zero, interrupting people who didn't want it. Yours lives on the left — the person was grateful to receive it. No billboard, no pre-roll, no banner can say that.

### Sell to the right advertisers — not to everyone

Here's one of the most common ways I've watched readers fail, and it has nothing to do with their pitch: they sold to the wrong advertisers. A great pitch aimed at the wrong brand fails every time, and it leaves the founder thinking the model is broken when really the match was wrong. Remember from the chapter on product as a medium: your ad space is worth reach × audience × frequency, and that tells you who your right advertiser is. If you're giving away free water to a broad public, your ideal advertisers are brands that want exactly that — broad reach and goodwill: local services, consumer brands, anyone who wants to look good in their community. If you pitch that same free water to a hyper-niche company chasing one narrow technical buyer, you'll fail, not because your pitch was weak, but because your medium doesn't reach their person. Before you write a single email, ask the Chapter 6 question about your own product: who actually ends up holding this, and which brands would pay to reach exactly those people? Sell to them.

### Pick your lane: warm inbound or focused outbound

This connects to a strategic choice that separates the people who close deals from the people who spray pitches into the void. There are two legitimate ways to bring in advertisers, and the winners commit to one — or run both deliberately — rather than being mediocre at both. The first lane is inbound. If your social content is strong, advertisers come to you, and these are the best leads you'll ever get, because they're pre-sold — they already saw the value and they're reaching out because they want in, so the sale is half-closed before you say a word. One good video can bring ten to a thousand leads to your site, some ready to buy on the spot, which is why you should eventually put an "order now" button right on your advertiser page. If you're a natural content machine, lean here. The second lane is focused outbound, and this is the one most people get wrong. The failure is cold-emailing every business in town with a generic pitch.

The success is the opposite: you find the specific category of advertiser that converts for your product — maybe it's real estate agents, maybe gyms, maybe hotels — and then you concentrate your outbound on more advertisers exactly like the ones who already said yes. That's advertiser-product fit, replicated. You're not spraying; you found your niche and you're drilling it. If you're better one-on-one than on camera, this is your lane. The lesson underneath both: focus beats breadth. Don't try to sell to the whole world. Either build a content engine that brings warm leads to you or find the niche that works and go deep — but stop spraying a generic pitch at everyone.

### Become a real student of the advertising industry

Now the hard truth about why some readers of the first edition failed, and it's worth hearing plainly: they tried to sell advertising while knowing nothing about advertising except what was in this one book. And then they sat across from a marketing director or investor who's done this for twenty years and got eaten alive. This book hands you a powerful new product to sell. It does not make you an expert in advertising — a complex, century-old industry with its own language, math, and expectations. So, treat this book as the start of your education, not the finish line. At a bare minimum, go get the free Google certifications — Google Ads and Google Analytics — so you understand how media buying, targeting, and measurement work. Beyond that, become a genuine student of the craft: learn what a CPM is, how brands think about return, what they measure. The readers who succeed are the ones who kept learning after they closed this book. The ones who failed treated one book as a complete education in a field that takes years to understand. Don't be that person. Your confidence in the room comes from competence, and competence comes from study.

### When your product gives more, charge more

Here's where most people get the pricing instinct exactly backwards. When your product is negatively priced — free and giving something extra, like a donation — you'd think you should charge less. You should charge more. Because you're no longer selling ad space. You're selling impact.

For a lot of brands, especially the ones trying to improve their image, impact is worth more than any click-through rate. When a brand places a large order, you can show them how many people they'll reach, how much goes to charity, and how many lives they're helping change — and in many cases, that alone is why they say yes. Some brands don't even ask about impressions. Netflix didn't even want a QR code on their bottles. They just wanted to do good and look good doing it, and that's worth a premium. And this points at the single most important idea in this whole chapter, the reason I built this in the first place. One day, someone in your town may offer a product like yours but donate more than you. What will you do then? You'll have to become more efficient, take better care of your customers, give more back. Sit with how different that is from normal business. Normally, companies compete to see who can be greedier. But past free, in the negative-pricing zone, the only way to compete is to see who can take the best care of their customers, their community, and the environment. That's the competition we should have been having all along — and your pricing is where you choose to join it.

### Sales skill is real — but anchor your price to real value

Now let me tell you something I've seen that cuts two ways. I've watched people sell ad space on coffee cans and pasta boxes for up to four dollars a unit — far more than I think that medium is worth without real targeting. How? They were simply better salespeople. There are two lessons in that, and you need both. The first is empowering: sales skill itself is enormously valuable, often worth more than the product's "fair" price. A great salesperson commands multiples of what a mediocre one does for the identical medium, so becoming a better salesperson is one of the highestleverage things you can do — selling is a craft, and like advertising, it rewards study and practice. The second lesson is the guardrail. A price that outruns the value you deliver is fragile. If you sell a pasta-box ad for four dollars with no targeting and no measurable engagement, that advertiser may not see four dollars of return, may not renew, and may walk away feeling burned — and a burned advertiser hurts the reputation of the whole medium, not just you. Remember the User Test from Chapter 2: the value must be real, or you're selling hype, and hype doesn't renew. So, here's the sustainable version: develop elite sales skill and build the targeting and measurement — the QR data, the dashboards, the hyper-targeting from the vending and packaging chapters — that justifies a premium honestly. Then a high price isn't overcharging. It's a fair price for a genuinely more valuable, measurable medium, closed by someone skilled enough to sell it. That's the combination that lasts.

### "Why don't I just do it myself?"

Some advertisers will push back: "Why don't I just buy water from the store, slap my label on it, and hand it out myself?" Don't get defensive — answer calmly, because you know your value. It's illegal to rebrand and redistribute someone else's product as an ongoing business; that breaks food and beverage manufacturing laws, and a small A/B test is one thing but it's no way to run a real operation. It would also cost them more once you add up buying retail, removing labels, printing, applying, and their own time. They'd waste months testing unreliable suppliers — it took me six failed manufacturers to find a good one, as you read earlier, and they don't want that education. And most of all, they're not paying for water; they're paying for the entire ecosystem you've already built — the packaging, the design, the distribution, the content, the attention, the goodwill, and your social reach, where their ad gets featured too. Deliver that calmly and most of them get it. The ones who don't will try, fail, and come back.

### Your price grows with what you offer

What you can charge climbs steadily with how much you deliver, and it's worth seeing that progression clearly, because it's how you raise rates without ever feeling like you're overcharging. At the simplest level, you're selling placement alone — just your ad on the bottle, the real-world visibility and goodwill and human engagement that come with it. That deserves a fair, clean price. Add your social content on top — the filmed reactions, the posts that make the sponsor the hero — and you're worth more, because now they're getting a media campaign, not just a label. Add QR analytics and a dashboard, so the advertiser can see their scans and engagement, and you've climbed again, because you've turned goodwill into something measurable. And at the top, when you bundle the full package — placement, content, targeting, a dashboard, and digital retargeting across Facebook, Google, and YouTube — you're no longer selling ad space at all. You're a full-funnel ad agency selling performance and strategy, and that commands the highest rates, especially once you can show the data to back it. Each rung up that progression is a rung up in price, and the beauty of it is that you climb it as you grow — you don't need the dashboard or the retargeting on day one. You start by selling placement, you add layers as you build them, and your price rises right alongside what you can prove. There's also good news on cost: as you scale, your per-unit costs drop while your pricing power rises. At real volume, costs fall toward rough ranges — a few cents for a plastic bottle, somewhat more for aluminum, more again for glass — though treat those as ballpark figures that move with your supplier, your market, and your year. Companies like Coca-Cola, Pepsi, and Nestlé already operate beyond that scale, which is what makes the big picture so striking: if just one of them adopted this model and gave even a few cents per unit to charity, it would trigger one of the largest wealth-redistribution events in history — all from a new ad format. And what you charge for today is only the start. Right now it's ad placement, content, and analytics — a few revenue streams. As you stack more layers over time, the value of a single unit climbs, sometimes dramatically, and we'll get into just how far that goes later in the book. But for now, keep your eyes on the rung in front of you: your first sale.

### Name the fear, then ask for the sale

So, let's get you to that first sale, and let me name the thing standing in your way, because it isn't a lack of scripts. It's fear. You're going to be scared to say the price out loud, scared they'll say no, scared you're charging too much. Everyone is. The cure isn't confidence you don't have yet — it's the three things we just built: the benchmark, so your price isn't a guess; your growing competence, so you can hold the conversation; and the data you'll soon have, so you can raise rates with proof. Walk in with those and the fear shrinks to a manageable size.

Then keep your outreach simple, because nobody reads long emails from strangers. The principles behind every good pitch are the same: keep it short, get to the value fast, show social proof, mention the cause, and make one clear, easy ask. Here's each format, and notice they're all built from those same principles. A cold email: "Subject: Want to give away [product] and look good doing it? Hi [Name] — I run a project called [Brand], inspired by FreeWater. We give away [product] for free, paid for by advertising, and we're launching a campaign in [City]. Your brand would get real-world exposure, viral content, and built-in goodwill — plus a donation to [cause] if you'd like to support it. Open to a quick 10-minute call this week?" Short, value-fast, cause included, one ask. A DM, for warmer outreach: "Hey [Name]! Love what you're building at [Company]. I run a free-product project inspired by FreeWater — brands sponsor real-world giveaways and get viral content plus cause marketing in return. Want me to send a quick overview?" A phone or in-person pitch, thirty seconds: "Hi, I'm [Name], and I run a company that gives away [product] for free — and your brand could be on every unit. We film the reactions, we support a cause, and people love it. It's like a billboard people thank you for. Want to hear more?" If they say yes, book the meeting and stop selling — you've got the time you needed. And the single most important sentence in this chapter, for when they ask the price: do not apologize, do not waffle. Say it clean and confident: "It's [X] per unit, including distribution, content, and built-in goodwill — and because we film the reactions, your brand becomes the hero, not just another ad." If it includes a donation, add: "That also includes a built-in donation to [cause] per unit, which is exactly why people are so happy to receive it." The price is fair, it's anchored, and you know what it's worth. Say it like you mean it.

### Find your product-market fit

Here's what resolves the whole pricing question, so you don't have to feel certain about your price — you just have to test it. Product-market fit, simply, is when advertisers want what you're offering and keep coming back. To find it, talk to ten advertisers and gauge the responses. Ask what they'd pay. If you need to, offer the first campaign at break-even to prove the value — that's not weakness, it's buying yourself a case study. Then track everything: scans, videos, posts, leads. Once you've got real results in hand, you raise your rates with the only thing that makes raising rates easy — proof. You don't need to nail your price on day one. You need to test it, measure it, and let the data give you the confidence to charge what it's worth. That's the whole game: price from a number people already trust, sell to the right advertisers in the lane that fits you, keep studying the craft, charge more when you give more, back every premium with real value, and ask for the sale without apology. Do that and you're not begging anyone for anything. You're offering the world something better than another ad they'd have skipped anyway — and getting paid fairly to do it. Next, let's talk about the big leagues: how to land major brands and the agencies that can sell you to all of them.

## Chapter 15: Landing Big Brands and Agencies

Let me start with a truth that will save you a lot of wasted effort: no one at a major brand is waiting for your email. Cold-email Subway and you'll probably never hear back. Message McDonald's on Instagram and it's the same silence. Big brands are buried under pitches every single day, most of them garbage, and unless you already know someone on the inside or you're coming through a legitimate agency, your message gets deleted unread. So, here's the pivot you need to make, because the last chapter and this one are different games with different rules. Everything you just learned about hustle — the cold emails, the cold calls, the showing up in person — genuinely works for local and mid-size advertisers. Up here, at the Fortune 500 level, it mostly doesn't, because these companies are protected by gatekeepers and drowning in noise. You cannot grind your way into the big leagues. You must play a different game entirely, and it comes down to two moves: make yourself impossible to ignore and make yourself easy to buy. Then you use agencies to sell you at scale. Let's take each.

### Be impossible to ignore: Go viral first

This is the shortcut, and it's the smartest path to a big brand deal that exists. If your free product goes viral — and it's clear your ad space is valuable and publicfacing — big brands eventually find you. That's not theory; it's exactly what happened to us. We had no direct line to NBC, NASCAR, or Netflix. But our content performed, and once it crossed a few million views, their teams and the agencies who work with them started reaching out on their own. And they didn't just try it once. NBC placed three separate orders with us inside a single twelve-month period — a Fortune 500 media company, with every advertising channel on earth available to it, choosing this one again and again. That's the signal that actually matters in this business: not that a big brand tries you once, but that they come back. And they weren't doing it out of mild curiosity. They'd seen something they couldn't get anywhere else: real people smiling and saying "thank you" with a brand in their hand, viral content that made the sponsor look like the hero, and a fresh advertising format with no ad fatigue. That combination is magnetic to a big brand, and no cold email could ever manufacture it. This is the same inbound lane from the last chapter, just operating at the highest level. There, one good video brought you warm local leads. Here, a video that truly hits brings you Fortune 500 teams and their agencies — pre-sold, because they already watched the value play out on their own screen. So, the honest truth is this: if your social content lands, big brands and even celebrities will come to you. If it doesn't, you'll be stuck cold-pitching people who won't take the meeting. The content is the door. Make yourself impossible to ignore, and the gatekeepers come find you.

### Be easy to buy: the dashboard is your price of admission

Getting found is only half of it. When a big brand does come knocking, you must be something they're able to buy from — and that trips up more founders than the finding does. Here's the thing most people miss: Big brands don't run on warm fuzzies. They run on data. They need to track how many people scanned your QR codes, see where the product was distributed, measure how long people spent with their content, watch real-time engagement, and — critically — download a report they can forward to their boss to justify the spend. If you can't give them that, you're just another "cool project," and a cool project can't be bought by a corporation that answers to a board. If you can give them that, you're a media platform, which is exactly the kind of thing they already know how to buy. Data is the price of admission to the big leagues. The good news is you don't need to be a coder or build some elaborate platform. You just need a simple dashboard that tracks the right things and makes an advertiser feel confident. There are five metrics that big advertisers actually care about: units distributed, scans, engagement (time on landing pages, clicks on affiliate links, form fills, AR interaction), location data (general region or city, privacy-respecting), and charity or bonus impact (total donated or gifted per campaign, which they love for PR). A handful of optional extras — videos and posts created, estimated social reach, quotes and photos from real recipients, follow-up conversions — sweeten it further. That's it. Even a basic version of this makes your offer look dramatically more professional and closes far more easily, because it speaks the language they already buy in. And if you want to genuinely blow an advertiser away, add retargeting sync. When someone scans the QR code, you can connect it to their Facebook Pixel or Google Ad Tag, so the brand's digital ads then follow that person across the internet for days afterward. That turns a single physical interaction into a full digital funnel: the first touch is a free product and a smile, the second is the social content or website visit, and the third and beyond are the ads chasing them across Facebook, Google, and YouTube. You've become the bridge between the offline and online worlds. Even if the advertiser has no idea what a Pixel is, you just say, "we can make it so people who scan your ad start seeing your brand everywhere online," and they'll get it instantly, and they'll love it. You don't need fancy infrastructure to start. Google Sheets and Drive are clean, shareable, and free for the early days. Airtable, Notion, or Glide are a step more polished and easier for a non-technical client to read. And eventually, if you have the budget or a team, a branded custom dashboard can help close the biggest deals. But begin simple — the tracking matters far more than the polish. When you pitch it, say something like: "you'll get a live dashboard during the campaign and a final report after, with your reach, scans, engagement, donation totals, and even retargeting results if you want to extend it digitally." (As a reference, our own advertising deck lives at freewater.io/assets as of this writing — use it as a guide for building your own, since these things change over time.)

### What big brands care about

If you understand what's really going through a big brand's mind, you can make yourself exactly the thing they're looking for. They care about reach — how many people will see it. Reputation — will this make us look good. Data — can we measure it like our digital ads. Ease — can your system plug into what we already do. Impact — can we honestly say we gave back. And then one more that nobody admits but everybody feels: the cool factor. Most CMOs at major companies are replaced within about three years, so they're under real pressure to bring something fresh that makes them stand out — and a free, ad-funded, cause-driven product that generates viral content is exactly the kind of thing that makes a marketing leader look like a visionary. You're not just selling them a campaign. You're selling them a way to keep their job and look brilliant doing it. Which means your pitch can be simple and confident: "You're already spending money on billboards and digital ads that people skip. We give away a physical product with your ad on it, handed directly to real people — your brand in the palm of their hands — and every unit includes a charitable donation on your behalf. You get impressions, brand alignment, social content, and a fully trackable backend. And the best part? It makes people happy." That's the whole thing. You don't need a hundred slides; you need that, plus the proof and the dashboard behind it.

### Agencies: the force multiplier

Now the move that changes everything, because chasing big brands one by one is slow and brutal even when you're doing it right. The faster path — the one that makes your life dramatically easier — is to get ad agencies on your side. You don't have to pitch every brand individually if you can get into one agency's deck, their book of offerings, because once you're in it, they start pitching you to all their clients automatically. One relationship, and a professional salesforce sells your model for years. But the same rule applies: a busy agency won't take the meeting unless you've made noise or have a warm intro. And just as importantly, most founders pitch agencies completely wrong. They walk in and say, "here's a free product with a cause!" — and that lands flat, because it's not framed in terms the agency cares about.

### Why agencies say yes: pitch their margin, not your mission

Here's the centerpiece, the thing that flips an agency from gatekeeper to ally. An ad agency is a high-end middleman: their entire business is pitching ideas to brands, bundling services, and taking a cut — usually twenty to thirty percent — of everything they sell. That's not cynical, it's their model. So, your pitch should never be "here's a cause." It should be: "here's a brand-new ad format you can pitch to your clients and make money on at every single layer." And you mean it literally, because they profit from every piece of your model. If they're a creative agency, they bill for designing the packaging and the QR codes — top agencies charge hundreds of dollars an hour for that alone. If they run events, they layer in distribution, brand ambassadors, and street-team activations and charge for each. If you've built in Pixel tracking and retargeting, they manage the Facebook, Google, and YouTube campaigns and take their normal percentage on the ad spend. And they get paid, as always, for strategy, account management, decks, and timelines. Every layer of your model is a layer they bill on. So, the more of your product they sell, the more design hours, ad spend, influencer work, and strategy work they sell too. On top of all that, your negatively priced product hands them a dream pitch for their client: feel-good, purpose-driven marketing with real, receipted impact — and the agency gets the credit for bringing it to the table. So, what you tell them, clearly, is this: "This isn't an alternative to Facebook ads, YouTube, TV, billboards, or experiential marketing. It's a new channel you can pitch alongside all of them — and it helps you sell more of your creative, digital, and event services." Frame it as their new revenue line, and you stop being a gatekeeper's problem and become a gatekeeper's opportunity.

Getting in the door looks like the brand playbook: go viral so they've already seen you, make a polished one-sheet or mini deck, and use LinkedIn or a warm intro to reach the right person — usually the strategy or experiential lead. Keep the ask small and easy: "I'd love to be included in your roster of creative ad solutions — this helps your clients look great, do good, and opens new digital and content revenue for you too." Your one-sheet needs only the essentials: a one-sentence explanation (free, plus impact, plus ad-powered), high-res product photos, a couple of reaction videos, your key metrics, the revenue potential across creative and digital and distribution add-ons, any testimonials or press, and a clear call to action: "want us in your pitch deck?" And once you're in, stay in. Follow up every couple of weeks with new videos and fresh wins from other campaigns and make it dead easy for them to drop your work into client decks. Agencies run on ammunition — give them great creative and good stats, and they'll sell you for years without you lifting a finger.

### If you're the agency reading this

I promised on page one that this book was for the ad agency executive, and this is your chapter, so let me speak to you directly. Everything above describes a founder pitching you — but you're holding a head start most of them don't have. You already have the clients, the creative teams, the media-buying muscle, and the relationships. This is simply a new format you can add to what you already sell — one that makes your clients look genuinely good, delivers real measurable impact, and lets you bill at every layer, from design to distribution to managed digital spend. The founders in this book are spending months or years trying to build what you already have. You could be running campaigns next quarter. Being early to a brand-new ad medium that makes your clients heroes isn't a threat to your business. It's one of the best opportunities in it. So that's the big leagues, and it really does come down to this: you don't pitch your way into it. You make yourself impossible to ignore so the brands come to you, you make yourself easy to buy with the data they need to say yes, and then you hand the whole thing to agencies who'll sell you to everyone for years. Different game, different rules — and once you're playing it, it scales in ways cold emails never could. ## Chapter 16: Why Not Just Use Facebook or Google Ads?

The most common reason I see people fail to close ad deals isn't a weak product or a weak mission — it's that they freeze on one question. When I ask a struggling founder, "Why should someone advertise on your product instead of Facebook or Google?" they go blank. And if you don't have a real answer to that, you've already lost the deal, no matter how passionate you are or how good your bottle looks. This is exactly why I told you in the last chapter to become a genuine student of advertising — because this is the moment that study pays off. You have to be able to look an advertiser in the eye and explain precisely why your medium works better, or at least differently, than the tools they already use. So, let me give you that answer, along with the answer to the deeper question every skeptic eventually asks: if everything becomes free, who's going to pay for any of it?

### It's a new tool, not a replacement

Here's the framing that wins, and it's a maturity move most beginners miss. Every advertising platform is just a tool. Billboards, TV, social media, Google search — they're all tools, and each one does certain jobs better or worse depending on the task. Your free product is simply a new tool: a hybrid of physical advertising, product sampling, and real-world engagement, with one quality none of the others have. It can't be ignored, skipped, or scrolled past. You're not asking someone to click on anything. You're putting a brand directly into someone's hand. That's the whole power, and it's a different power than a search ad has. The reason this framing matters is that it stops you from sounding like a zealot. You are not telling advertisers to abandon Facebook and Google — that's not the point, and saying it would make you sound naïve. You're handing them one more tool that's better for certain situations, and the honest truth is that your product,

Facebook, and Google all perform best when they're combined intelligently. That answer makes you sound like someone who understands the industry, which is exactly who advertisers want to buy from.

### Why it beats the incumbents at certain jobs

Once you've framed it as a different tool, you can show where your tool actually wins — and the trick is to frame every advantage as a contrast with what Facebook and Google can't do. Your product is useful, so nobody throws it away. People carry it around, put it in the fridge, hand it to a friend — that's multiple impressions from a single unit, with no extra spend. A digital ad gets one fleeting moment; your bottle keeps working for days. Your product makes people feel good, because you're not just selling an ad, you're selling purpose and real impact, and that changes how people feel about the advertiser — try buying that emotion with a banner. With your product, the message isn't buried in a feed; it's front and center, every sip, every glance, every time it sits on a desk or gets passed around at an event. For local targeting in particular, once your costs and sponsors are in place, many advertisers can get more impressions per dollar than they would online — though that depends on the campaign, and it's strongest for local, real-world reach. And if you share the space with the right non-competing brands, you can even subsidize it down to nothing. Above all, your tool has a physical, real-world presence that the digital giants simply cannot reach. That last point is where the contrast gets decisive, so make it vivid. Want to reach people walking around a music festival? Waiting in a dentist's office? Shopping downtown on a Saturday afternoon? Standing somewhere with no cell signal at all? Good luck doing any of that with a search ad. But a free product placed in their hand, with a message on it and a little gift or donation attached — that's something people are genuinely happy to receive. And here's the line that closes it: when was the last time you heard anyone say they love Facebook or Google ads? Never. Nobody has ever loved a banner ad. That's precisely why this works, and that's how you explain it.

### If everything is free, who's going to advertise?

Now the deeper doubt, the one that goes past any single sale and questions whether the whole future you're describing is even real. It's one of the most common questions I get, and one of the easiest to answer — because the answer is hiding in plain sight. Look at Google, Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and the entire video-game industry. Many of the most valuable advertising businesses in history are built on exactly this: giving users free access and monetizing their attention. They aren't charging you for the product — they're giving it away and making billions through advertising, freemium models, and data. So, the premise that "free kills advertising" is exactly backwards. Some of the most valuable companies in the world are already built on giving things away and monetizing the attention that follows. In a free or negatively priced economy, advertisers don't disappear — they double down. Because the moment you're giving away something people need every single day — water, food, hygiene products, electricity — your product becomes one of the most valuable advertising surfaces in the world. Advertisers already gravitate toward free platforms precisely because free drives the engagement, the data, and the results they're chasing. Free doesn't make advertising obsolete. It makes advertising essential. And it's worth glancing, briefly, at where that could lead. As more products become free, donation-backed, or even pay the user, competition between brands probably doesn't fade — it shifts character. Brands may start competing not just on price or flavor but on who gives the most, donates more, and treats people better. I think it's plausible that one day you'll see free Pepsi advertising against free Coke, or a negatively priced McDonald's against Burger King — the "compete by giving more" idea from Chapter 2, arriving at the scale of the whole economy. I won't pretend that future is guaranteed; it's where I believe this is heading. But you don't need it to be true to win today. The point that closes the deal is the grounded one: free makes advertising more essential, not less. So both questions a skeptic throws at you — "why you instead of Facebook?" and "who'll pay if everything's free?" — are really the same question underneath:

do you actually understand advertising? Now you do. Your medium is a new tool that wins where the digital giants can't reach, and free makes advertising more essential rather than less. That's not a defensive answer you mumble when cornered. It's a confident one you offer with a straight back. Next, let's make sure you can deliver all of this out loud — to a journalist, an investor, or a stranger at a party — without flinching.

## Chapter 17: How to Talk About Your Mission

You can have the best product, the deepest mission, and real-world impact — and still go nowhere if you can't deliver the message. Whether you're filming a TikTok, getting grilled by a journalist, pitching an investor, or chatting with someone at a party, it all comes down to one skill: being able to tell your story, cold, to anyone, anywhere, without flinching. That skill is how attention becomes belief, and belief is what turns a product into a movement. So, this chapter is about mastering it — the message itself, how to lock it in, and how to deliver it when the stakes are highest. Let's start with the message.

### The message: your one-minute pitch

If you do it right, one minute is all you need. A potential investor, a teammate, a journalist, a connector, someone you just met — a clean one-minute pitch is the fastest way to explain your idea and get a real response. Say the right thing to the right person and they'll either say "I'm in" or start asking questions, and both mean it's working. Here's the five-part formula that took me years to figure out. One — your one-sentence pitch. This has to be airtight: a complete, closedloop idea in a single sentence. "Our spring water is free because we use the packaging as advertising space." If your listener tunes out after this one sentence, they should still understand the entire concept. Two — your secret sauce. Briefly, what makes it special. "Free food and water are the only advertising mediums that can reach anyone on Earth — and we've combined that with real-time data and hyper-targeted delivery. This isn't just an ad. It's a movement." This is your differentiator, the thing that separates you from every gimmick they've heard before. Three — your traction, if you have it. If you've built any momentum, flex it now, because numbers open doors. "So far we've given away 100,000 free bottles and donated $10,000 to charity" — or, before launch, "we already have 500,000 followers and our biggest video hit 15 million views." Use whatever real numbers you have; just don't inflate them, because you'll have to back them up. Four — your mission sentence. This is where you hook their heart. "And it's not just free — we donate ten cents per bottle to fight the global water crisis. Imagine the impact if even 10% of the world drank one of these a day." This reminds them you're not just building a business; you're changing something. Five — your ask. Make it unmistakable what you need. "We're raising $500K for 10% equity." "We're looking for a CTO with mobile and logistics experience." "We're hiring a salesperson with CPG or ad-agency background." Don't dance around it — tell them exactly what you're looking for. That's the whole structure. Have a few versions ready for different audiences, because knowing who you're talking to and what they care about is half the battle.

### The mastery: memorize the questions, not just the answers

Now, how do you lock all of this in, so it never abandons you? Here's the counterintuitive trick that helped me most: don't start by memorizing your answers — start by mastering the questions you'll be asked. If you only rehearse a monologue, you'll sound like you're reciting one. But if you know the questions cold, you can have a real conversation with a real person while still delivering powerful, consistent answers every time. It's the same philosophy as the street interviews from earlier in the book — there, you asked the questions; here, you prepare for the ones coming at you. The questions almost always fall into the same buckets: What is it? Why is it free? Who pays for it? Why would a brand want this? Is it legal? Is it sustainable? Why hasn't this been done before? What happens if it goes mainstream? Why are you the person to do this? And how can I get involved? Write your answer to each, refine it, and then drill it until it flows without thought. And here's how you drill it under pressure — I call it the car test. Get in your car, turn the radio all the way up, and recite your pitch and your answers louder than the radio, over and over, until you can deliver every word smoothly and confidently while distracted. It forces your brain to stay locked in amid chaos, and if you can stay sharp yelling over noise, you'll be ready for anything — cameras, podcasts, a hostile interview, a surprise question from an investor. The bar I hold myself to: if someone burst into my room at 3am and threw a bucket of water in my face, I should be able to stand up, look them in the eye, and nail my pitch. Drill it until you're that unshakeable.

### The principle: don't sound rehearsed

Here's the paradox that ties the message and the mastery together: memorizing your material should make you sound more natural, not robotic. The whole point of drilling it is to know it so thoroughly that it comes out sounding spontaneous, even though it isn't. So be conversational, let your energy show, and adjust your register to the room — relax for a podcast, tighten up for a boardroom, loosen up at a party — but always hit the same core truths underneath. Consistency of message with authenticity of delivery is the combination, and it's what makes people believe you. Which brings us to the highest stakes place you'll ever deliver it.

### The performance: handling the press

If you're doing this right, the press will eventually come knocking — a local news story, a podcast, a popular blog, and if you keep going, national and maybe global media. Be ready, because press is a double-edged sword: it can amplify your mission or derail it, depending entirely on how you handle it. The first rule is to keep the focus on the mission, not on yourself. This isn't your moment, it's the mission's moment, so don't spend the interview talking about how clever your product is or how cool your company sounds. Talk about why — the problem you're solving and why it matters now. Just like your best videos, let the truth do the work and let the impact speak.

The second move flips the whole energy: ask the host the same thoughtprovoking questions that made your content go viral. Do you think water should be free? What's the most you've ever paid for a bottle? If water is free, what else could be? This gets the journalist personally involved, and it turns the segment emotional, reflective, and shareable instead of a dry Q&A. What if the host gets critical and tries to poke holes? It's rare, but it happens, and it's where all your drilling pays off — you stay calm and lean on the truth: "This model is already working — it's been done in the US, Germany, Spain, Pakistan, India, and beyond, with some of the biggest global brands. It's not theory. It's just new to you." You've already got the answers to every skeptic's question from the chapter on Facebook and Google — who pays if it's free, what's the catch, why this isn't idealistic — so this isn't about scrambling for new material; it's about delivering what you already know, under pressure, without losing your calm. There's one skeptic question worth adding here, because it comes up most with the press: "Isn't there already too much advertising?" The answer is clean and it reframes the whole thing. The world already produces trillions of packaged goods a year, and nearly all of them are already covered in ads — it's just that the ad is for the brand itself. We're simply replacing that self-promotion with a thirdparty sponsor — a Toyota ad on a bottle of water — and using the value to cover costs and fund donations. It's not more ads. It's smarter ones. Then close the segment with something bigger than you — a what-if that leaves people thinking. "Just imagine if 10% of our country drank one free bottle a day. We'd send millions of dollars to charity every year, without a single penny of taxpayer money." Make them believe in something better and genuinely possible. That's how you turn a press hit into momentum. And the moment it airs, download the clip, edit it, and repost it across all your channels immediately.

### The payoff

Everything in this chapter comes down to one equation: when your message is consistent and your delivery is real, people believe you — and belief is what turns a free product into a movement. You don't need to be a polished speaker or a marketing expert. You need to know your story cold and deliver it like you mean it, whether that's to a camera, a boardroom, a skeptical journalist, or a stranger at dinner who might change your life. One last practical habit: once a month, film your one-minute pitch properly and post it. Some of mine have pulled in millions of views, and you genuinely never know who's watching — an investor, a journalist, a future partner, a giant brand. Master the message, drill it until it's unshakeable, deliver it with real energy, and put it out into the world consistently. That's how you talk about your mission — and how the mission grows beyond you. Next, we scale — starting with a way to deliver physical goods so efficiently it beats Amazon at its own game.

## Chapter 18: The Power of Consecutive Delivery

Let me show you a way to deliver physical goods so efficiently that it beats Amazon at its own game — and then let me show you why almost no one on Earth can actually use it. Shipping bulky things like water and food is brutally expensive and bad for the environment. But imagine a truck that pulls onto a street and drops a full 24-pack at every single house, one after another after another, from a small facility right around the corner — never skipping a door, never collecting a payment, never handling a return. The cost per delivery collapses, the impact climbs, and everyone wins. That's consecutive delivery, and it's one of the most important pieces of this entire model at scale. Here's the part that matters most, and it's the whole reason this chapter exists. You cannot run consecutive delivery profitably if you sell things. Think about why. Amazon can't send a truck down a street stopping at every house, because not every house ordered something, and the ones that did require a transaction and the possibility of a return at each stop. All of that friction makes house-after-house delivery uneconomical for anyone operating on the right side of zero. But the moment your product is free, that friction vanishes — every door becomes a stop, there's no money to collect and no return to manage, and the route hits an efficiency a paid model simply can't reach. Profitable consecutive delivery doesn't switch on because you're clever. It switches on at zero. And it's the number line from Chapter 2 made physical: at free, the delivery becomes viable, and the further you push into negative pricing, the more profitable the route becomes.

### How it works

You load a vehicle with personalized 24-packs — water, or whatever you're giving away — each case tailored to a specific household by size, preferences, and past behavior. Then you run the route: a case every week or two to a single-person home, a few cases a month to a big family, only ever what's needed, so nothing is wasted. You don't need to be Amazon to do this. A small van works. So does a cargo bike, or a few local volunteers. All that matters is that the route is efficient and repeatable. Finish the neighborhood, reload, move to the next. And here's why the expensive part of shipping — the long-distance haul — disappears entirely: you're not shipping water across the country. You make it locally, in a small facility near the people you serve, then deliver it down the street. When production sits next to the consumer, there's no freight across state lines to pay for. You've replaced the single most expensive part of physical distribution with a short drive around the block. By combining consecutive delivery with bottle return, each household receives new cases of free water as empty bottles are collected in the same stop—dramatically reducing both distribution and per-unit packaging costs.

### Every case is made just for them

The magic is in the personalization, and it's also why every door is worth stopping at. Each 24-pack is built for that specific household across three layers. The first is the physical packaging — every bottle and the outer box printed with ads chosen for that person or family. The second is a QR code or anchor on each unit that opens a digital door. And the third is the augmented reality or digital layer that code launches — videos, apps, offers, custom content made for them. The whole delivery feels like it was made for that household, because it was. That's what justifies hitting every house on the street: you're not dropping generic product, you're delivering something individualized to each home.

### The further left you go, the more profitable it gets

Now watch the route walk left along the number line, because this is where it stops merely breaking even and starts paying for itself several times over. At free, the delivery is viable. Print ads on the packaging, and now those ads are funding the product and the drop-off — you've moved further negative. Then wrap the vehicle itself in ads, the way we talked about in the logistics chapter: the van becomes a moving billboard, the bike gets a sign, the cart sells its surface, and in a dense market that income can cover more than the entire cost of shipping over time. That's negatively priced freight — your delivery vehicle now pays you to make the trip. Push further still with the digital layers, the scans and the offers, and the margin deepens again. And at the far end, the person receiving the case can be compensated too — a direct reward, or a donation triggered to charity simply for accepting the delivery. Each step to the left makes the whole loop more profitable. You're building a delivery system that, taken to its full depth, pays everyone involved.

### It compounds

And it gets better every single loop, because consecutive delivery isn't a static route — it's a system that improves itself. Each pass collects better data on what each household actually wants. Better data sharpens your targeting. Sharper targeting makes your ad space more valuable. More valuable ad space lowers your effective cost and funds more delivery. Around it goes, tighter and cheaper each cycle. You're not just handing out free product. You're building a smart, local, personalized ad network, one 24-pack and one doorstep at a time, that gets stronger the longer it runs.

### The proof: SXSW 2022

This isn't theory — we tested it. At South by Southwest in Austin in 2022, we loaded the FreeWater truck and hit a neighborhood with a team of three plus a driver. In sixty minutes, we delivered 4,800 bottles door to door — that's 200 full 24-packs. If each person were paid ten cents a bottle, that works out to $120 per person for that hour — but read that as the implied labor value of one specific test, not a guaranteed hourly wage. Still, in the right area, this could genuinely become one of the better-paying delivery jobs around, especially for people without traditional degrees or experience. Here's the kicker. If we'd shipped those same 24-packs through traditional carriers, it would have cost roughly $10 a case even at massive scale, and up to $35 or more at the volume we were testing. It's expensive because each 24-pack weighs around 25 pounds, and conventional shippers don't run consecutive routes or sell ad space on their trucks to bring the price down. On top of that, as much as 10% of those packages would likely have arrived damaged — and a dented bottle with a smudged ad doesn't sell anything. By delivering them ourselves, consecutively, house by house on a consistent route, we saved thousands of dollars and created real earning potential for the team, all at once.

### One free route makes paid shipping cheaper, too

Here's a bonus most people miss. Once you've got a consistent route for a single free product, you can offer cheaper shipping for other, for-sale goods — because you're already going there. Picture it: your delivery stops at the Clark family's house every Tuesday morning like clockwork, and Mr. Clark just ordered the latest iPhone from Apple. Apple can save money by splitting the delivery fee with you, because you're already heading to that doorstep in a far more efficient way than they ever could. Your free-product route quietly becomes low-cost infrastructure that paid goods can ride along on.

### Where this could go

I'll be honest that this last part is where I'm describing a direction, not a guarantee. But if you take this model to its full potential, I believe the delivery company itself can become negatively priced — earning money by delivering free products, with the ads on the packaging, the vehicle, and the digital layers more than covering every drop. Once consistent free water deliveries are running, it gets easy to layer on free beverages, snacks, and other essentials on the same route. In time, and especially once autonomous vehicles and humanoid robots enter the picture and drop the cost further, I think this evolves into something genuinely new: a negatively priced grocery and product delivery service, where the more people receive, the more impact you create. That's the vision. You don't need it to be true to make the practical version work today — but it's where the road leads.

### Can the giants copy this?

So, can the big players follow you into consecutive delivery? They can — and I genuinely hope they do, because a giant adopting this would do enormous good and would mean the mission is winning. But they face different versions of the same hurdle, and it's worth understanding both, because it tells you where your real edge is. The companies that already deal in physical goods — Coca-Cola, Pepsi, Nestlé, Amazon, the big shippers — aren't stopped by an inability to cross zero. They're stopped by what they'd have to tear down to do it well. Their factories, trucks, and warehouses were all built and optimized for the paid, transactional world. To reach the deepest, most efficient, most profitable version of consecutive delivery, they'd have to build new physical infrastructure designed around free and negative pricing from the ground up — which is slow, expensive, and cuts against the entire operation they spent decades perfecting. Their existing infrastructure isn't a head start here. In a way, it's the wrong infrastructure, and that's the real barrier for them. Then there are the digital advertising giants — Google, Facebook — and they're in the most interesting position of all. They already own the two hardest pieces to build: millions of advertiser relationships and the ability to make hyper-targeted ads at massive scale. What they don't have is physical manufacturing and distribution — but unlike Coke, they're not weighed down by the wrong physical infrastructure either, because they have none to unwind. That means they could go straight to the newest, most efficient ways to manufacture and distribute — micro-factories, consecutive delivery, print-on-demand — and bolt their existing advertiser base and targeting engine directly onto a brand-new physical layer built correctly from day one. If anyone moves fastest into this, it might actually be them. So, your edge was never that the giants can't follow. It's the head start you have while they realize they need to, the years it takes the physical players to rebuild and the digital players to move into the physical world — and the fact that even when they do follow, the mission has won. That's the kind of competition this was always meant to create. And all of it starts with one free 24-pack, one house, one route. Then you just keep going, door after door after door. Next, let's finally get into the thing I've promised you since the very beginning: the data that quietly powers every piece of this.

## Chapter 19: Honest Data — The Hidden Power of Free

I've been promising you since early in this book that the data your free products generate is one of the most valuable things you'll ever build. This is the chapter where I show you why. But to understand what makes it so powerful, you first have to understand a problem hiding in plain sight: almost all the consumer data in the world today is a lie. Picture yourself walking into a grocery store with twenty dollars for fruit. Bananas are fifty cents a pound. Mangoes are three dollars each. Blueberries are six dollars a pint. You walk out with a bunch of bananas and maybe two pears. The store's system records the purchase, adds it to your profile, blends it with millions of others, and concludes: this customer prefers bananas and pears. And that conclusion is completely false. You wanted the mangoes. You wanted the blueberries. What the store recorded wasn't your preference — it was your budget. Every choice in that cart was shaped by the prices on the labels, not by what you actually wanted to eat. Now multiply that by billions of shoppers and trillions of transactions across every category on Earth, and you see the scale of the problem. Most consumer data in the world doesn't measure what people want. It measures what people can afford. The two are not the same — and most failed product launches and missed predictions are downstream of confusing them.

### What honest data is

Honest data is the signal you get when a person's choice is made without financial pressure. It isn't a technology or a platform or a collection method. It's a quality of information that only appears under specific conditions — conditions that have been rare across all of human history, because almost everything has always had a price. When a product is free, choosing it is no longer a compromise between what you want and what you can afford. And when a product is better than free — when taking it sends value to you, your community, or a cause you care about — the choice becomes even more expressive. Selection reveals what you prefer. Participation reveals what you're committed to. Feedback reveals your context. Strip away the price, and for the first time the data starts telling the truth about what human beings actually want. That's honest data, and once you're distributing free and negatively priced goods at scale, you produce it as a natural byproduct of giving things away.

That's the standalone idea. Now here's the part that makes it genuinely revolutionary, because honest data isn't one thing — it comes in tiers.

### The Five Tiers of Data

Every dataset in the economy can be sorted into one of five tiers, based not on the technology used to collect it, but on the economic conditions under which the choice was made. Two of the tiers are the contaminated data the world runs on today. The other three are honest — and they've barely existed until now. Think of it as a ladder, each rung capturing a truer or richer kind of signal. Tier 1 — Constrained data. What people do under financial pressure. This is the grocery cart from a moment ago: the data mixes what you wanted with what you could afford, and there's no way to separate the two after the fact. Almost all consumer data today — purchases, surveys, ad clicks, app downloads — lives in this tier. A dataset can be enormous, clean, and beautifully organized and still fundamentally misrepresent what people actually want, because the choice underneath it was shaped by price. Tier 2 — Compliance data. What people do under "free, but only if…" arrangements. Imagine a bag of chips that's free, but only if you scan five QR codes and watch an ad for each — otherwise it's two dollars. A person who scans all five and takes the chips is not telling you they wanted to watch five ads. They're telling you they wanted free chips badly enough to put up with the gate. The system records five scans and five completions, all real behavior — but it's not honest. It's measuring friction tolerance, not desire. Most "free with engagement" setups live here, and it's an easy trap to fall into yourself: don't mistake compliance with your gates for genuine preference. Tier 3 — Revealed preference. The first honest tier. What people choose when everything is free, with no conditions and no friction. Now the same shopper walks into a market where every fruit is free — pick what you want, take it home, eat it. They take mangoes and blueberries. Pears sometimes. Bananas never again. That signal is what the person actually wants to eat, full stop — no budget, no gate, nothing else mixed in. This is honest data in its simplest form, and where it appears today — libraries, free public services — the results routinely contradict what the same people's purchase data predicted. People reach for things they'd never pay for and ignore things they buy every week. Tier 4 — Values-weighted preference. What people choose when products are negatively priced and the causes vary. Here's an example: I don't much like bananas, and mangoes are my favorite, but if the mango is free while a slightly mushy banana donates fifteen cents to help kids with cancer, I might take the banana a certain percentage of the time. When I do, the data isn't capturing "this person prefers bananas." It's capturing my whole priority structure — how I weigh taste against cause, donation size against preference. That's a richer signal than Tier 3, a window into what people value, not just what they want to consume. It has to be read carefully — a producer who mistakes it for simple fruit preference draws the wrong conclusion — but read correctly, it's a kind of signal no system has ever produced at scale. Tier 5 — Brand commitment. The loudest signal a data system can capture: what people pay for when free and negatively priced alternatives are sitting right next to it. Picture a market with free fruit, negatively priced products that donate to causes, even options that pay you to take them — and a machine selling Pepsi for two dollars a can. The shopper walks past everything free, walks past the cola that donates, walks past the juice that pays them, and buys the two-dollar Pepsi anyway. That choice strips away every excuse — no switching cost, no habit, no unawareness, because every alternative was right there, free or better than free. What's left is pure brand attachment. It's the cleanest possible evidence that a brand is genuinely worth what it charges, and no system in today's economy can capture it. Remember the teaser back in the vending chapter — the machine that captures "the single most valuable signal there is" when someone pays for a premium bottle while free ones sit beside it? This is that signal. That's Tier 5, and a properly built negatively priced machine or store is one of the only things on Earth that can capture it cleanly. So, there's the ladder. Tiers 1 and 2 are the contaminated data the world runs on now. Tiers 3, 4, and 5 are the honest data the free model makes possible — three brand-new categories of truthful signal. Tier 3 tells you what people want. Tier 4 tells you what they value. Tier 5 tells you what they'll pay for even when paying is the most expensive option in the room. The current economy can produce none of these at scale, because it has never given people the conditions under which they become possible. You can.

### Signal, not volume

This is also what makes honest data fundamentally different from the "big data" that makes people nervous. Traditional big data chases volume — collect as much behavioral information as possible and hope useful patterns fall out of the pile. But the assumption that more data is better data is wrong. Much of what's collected today is noise: bot traffic, ad fraud, engagement farming, and beneath it all, mostly Tier 1 — the same price-distorted signal that's misled companies for a century. Enormous computing power gets spent analyzing oceans of contaminated data, hoping quantity will overcome the lie. Honest data flips that. It's not about quantity; it's about truth. A smaller amount of data that's telling you what people want is worth more than a billion data points shaped by price and fraud. You're not drowning in noise like everyone else. You're holding signal.

### You own your data — and that's the whole difference

Now, the part that matters most ethically, because the moment people hear "data," they think of being tracked and harvested. Honest data is the opposite of surveillance capitalism, and here's the line that separates them: this data is contributed, not extracted. You chose to take a free product. You chose to scan, or interact, and you got something real in return — and because you gave it rather than having it taken from you in the background, it belongs to you and your community, not to a platform mining you for profit. This is the User Test from Chapter 2 made concrete, and the consent principle from the QR chapter taken to its conclusion. Facebook's data is extracted — you're the product, and you didn't come out ahead. Honest data is the inverse: the person came out ahead, they knew exactly what they were sharing and why, and the value flows back to them. That's not a small distinction. It's the entire difference between building the truth layer of a new economy and building another machine for spying on people. Hold that line, because it's what makes honest data something the world should want to exist.

### What it unlocks

When manufacturers and brands can finally see honest demand, the whole economy stops guessing. They make the right amount of the right things. They stop producing what nobody actually wanted, stop overproducing, stop paying to store and eventually landfill the excess, and start responding to real demand instead of marketing hunches and accounting projections. Imagine a brand that can see families in one neighborhood genuinely choose orange juice every Monday — they don't guess at flavors or volumes, they just follow the truth, ship exactly what's wanted, and waste almost nothing. That isn't only good for business. It's good for the planet. Less overproduction, less wasted energy, less overstock rotting in warehouses, less landfill. More of what people actually want, less of what they don't. Honest data is one of the rare things that makes the economy more efficient and the world cleaner at the same time.

### The hidden engine

Here's why I've been pointing at this since the beginning. The free product is what people see. The donation is what they feel. But honest data might be the most valuable thing you quietly produce — because it's the first truthful picture of human demand that has ever existed at scale. Every business in history has had to guess what people want, distorted by price at every turn. You're building the system that finally knows. You're not just giving things away. You're generating the signal that lets the whole economy stop wasting, stop guessing, and start making the right things for the right people — while the data stays in the hands of the people who created it. That's the hidden power of free. Next, let's look at where all of this is heading.

## Chapter 20: The Future Is Free, Fast, and Local

Most people never think about where their bottle of water actually comes from. But trace its real journey and you'll find something close to madness — and once you see it, you'll understand why the free model doesn't just change how products get paid for. It collapses the entire insane supply chain into something local, fast, and almost waste-free.

### The 20,000-mile bottle

Take a bottle of premium water — a Fiji, say. Crude oil gets drilled, often in the Middle East. That oil is shipped somewhere else to be refined. The refined product typically travels to China to be turned into plastic pellets. Those pellets are shipped to Fiji, where they're blown into bottles. The bottles are filled with water, and then that water — one of the heaviest, cheapest substances on Earth — is shipped across the entire planet. It sits in a series of warehouses for months, sometimes more than a year. Then it rests on a store shelf, gets bought, gets opened, and, if you're lucky, lands in a recycling bin. By the time you count the journey of the oil, the pellets, the bottle, and the water itself, that single bottle has easily traveled more than 20,000 miles before you ever twist the cap. That's an educated estimate, not a precise odometer reading — but it's the right order of magnitude, and it's deranged. And the waste is worse than the distance. The OECD estimates that less than 10% of all plastic ever actually gets recycled — the rest goes to landfills, gets burned, or leaks into rivers and oceans. So most of those globe-trotting bottles end their lives as pollution. And here's the part that should really stop you: a 2024 study from Columbia and Rutgers found that a single liter of bottled water contains, on average, around 240,000 tiny plastic particles, the vast majority of them nanoplastics small enough to pass through your gut and lungs and into your bloodstream and organs — and that's true even of the expensive brands. We ship water around the world at enormous cost, destroy the environment to do it, and drink plastic in the process. We overpay to harm the planet and ourselves at the same time.

### This is everything, not just water

And it isn't only water. The same global madness is baked into nearly every product you touch — the aluminum in your soda and beer, the wrappers on your snacks, the cardboard, the electronics, the furniture, the clothing, even the food ingredients themselves. Most modern supply chains are a maze of international stops, processing and reprocessing, layer upon layer of packaging, and repeated shipping — all just to land something on a shelf in the hope that someone buys it. The whole system is built for complexity, not efficiency. It's built to sell, not to last. And it's almost completely disconnected from the person at the end of it. The free model fixes this by collapsing two things at once: the distance and the storage.

### Collapsing the distance: make it local

Here's the better way. Instead of shipping water across the planet, you make a reusable glass bottle, fill it with clean local water, and — this is the part most people don't realize is possible — you copy the exact chemical and mineral profile of a premium brand right there in a lab. You print a meaningful ad on the bottle, distribute it locally to the right person, and then, after they use it, you collect it, clean it, refill it, and run the loop again, generating fresh ad revenue every cycle. You've just deleted the recklessly long supply chain, cut the waste, cut the microplastics, and built a clean, profitable, local loop. "But premium water tastes better," someone will say — and there's a real answer. Fiji and Evian have distinctive mineral balances, that's true. But the composition of any premium water can be replicated: take local water, purify it, add the same minerals, and done well, most people can't reliably tell the difference in a blind taste. You get the luxury quality without the plastic, the shipping, or the storage. And this same logic applies to most of what's sitting in your local supermarket — even the packaging can increasingly be made locally from hemp and other plantbased materials. Some things will always need their origin; coffee is going to keep coming from coffee-growing regions. But the rest? The rest can be local. And for the planet's sake, it needs to be.

### Collapsing the storage: your pantry is the new warehouse

The second thing the free model collapses is storage — and this is where honest data and consecutive delivery, the two ideas from the last chapters, finally pay off together. In the traditional system, brands overproduce, ship to warehouses, ship again to stores, and then pray it sells before it expires. It's slow, it's wasteful, and it's a big reason the UN estimates that roughly a third of all the food the world produces is lost or wasted — much of it spoiling somewhere in that long chain or sitting too long on a shelf. But you don't have to guess anymore. With honest data flowing in, you already know what people want, how much, and when — so you make the right amount in small local bursts instead of overproducing "just in case." And with consecutive delivery, you send it straight to people's doors. There's simply no reason to warehouse things for months. The most efficient storage system in the world already exists, and it's been there all along: people's fridges, pantries, counters, and cabinets. When you deliver direct, house by house, the product is already exactly where it needs to be. The clock on its freshness starts when someone actually uses it, not when it's boxed up in a depot. More freshness, less spoilage, and the giant warehouse quietly becomes obsolete.

### For ad-supported products, speed isn't just green — it's profit

Here's the angle that makes this matter to your bottom line, not just your conscience. When your product is ad-supported, it's only valuable if it reaches the audience in time. Nobody wants to pay for an ad that's sitting in a dusty warehouse, or for a campaign that expires before the bottle ever hits someone's hand. So every day a product spends in storage is a wasted impression — a lost piece of the very thing the advertiser paid for. Speed, in this model, is money. The faster and more locally you produce and deliver, the more impressions you capture, the happier your advertisers are, and the better your economics get. Going local and warehouse-free isn't a sustainability nicety you bolt on; it's a direct upgrade to how well the whole machine pays. And notice this gives you an edge you can use right now, not just in some distant future. Producing locally means fresher product, lower freight costs — recall the negatively priced freight from the logistics chapter — less spoilage, and a genuinely great story to sell to advertisers, who increasingly want to be associated with something sustainable and local. Local isn't only where this is heading. It's a competitive advantage available today.

### Where this is heading

Step back, and you can see the shape of it. The future isn't a landscape of megawarehouses and bottles that circle the globe twice. I believe it's the opposite: micro-manufacturing close to the consumer, hyper-local fulfillment, and ordinary homes acting as the smart endpoints of the whole system. Honest data tells you exactly what to make. Consecutive delivery gets it there fresh. Local production strips out the distance, the middlemen, the warehouse bills, and the needless freight. I won't pretend every product crosses over to this model tomorrow, or that the global supply chain unwinds overnight — it won't. But the direction is clear, and the pull is simple: local is cleaner, fresher, and cheaper all at once. That's the quiet power in all of this. The free model was never only about giving things away. It's about making the right things, near the people who want them, and delivering them before they ever go stale — keeping the profits local, the waste low, and the quality as high or higher than what crossed an ocean to reach you. Free works best when it's fast and local. And once you've got honest data and consecutive delivery running, you're already most of the way there. Next, for everyone whose country or budget slams the factory door shut: you never needed the factory at all.

## Chapter 21: No Factory? No Problem

In some countries and cities, you simply can't hire a co-packer to make a product for you unless you own the factory outright. If you wanted to bottle ad-supported water or juice or food, you'd have to build an entire production facility yourself — expensive and risky, especially at the start. If that's your situation, it would be easy to assume the whole model is closed to you. It isn't. And the reason is worth pausing on, because it reframes everything in this chapter. You never actually needed a factory. What you needed was ad-funded value delivered to people — that's what this whole model is. The bottle was only ever one vehicle for it. Take the bottle away and the model is still completely intact, because there are several ways to deliver free, ad-funded, even negatively priced value with no manufacturing at all. And I'll be honest up front about something I've come to believe: one of these paths might actually be easier to scale than bottles ever were. Let me give you three, starting with that one.

### The headline path: the digital water dispenser

Instead of making a physical product, you make a machine that gives water away. You build or retrofit a dispenser about the size of a vending machine, connect it directly to the city's tap supply, and dispense filtered, purified water for free. The front — and optionally the sides — are digital screens, exactly like the TV-style billboards already bolted to buildings in every city. You've now created a free water experience, a digital billboard advertisers can rent, and a clean way around the manufacturing laws all at once. The water comes from the city and people refill their own bottles, so there's no co-packing to be illegal in the first place. This is the same machine we reached in the manufacturing chapter when the factory walls got too high, and the same screen-funded model as Hope Hydration's stations — so you already know the hardware story. What I want to add here is why I'd seriously consider leading with it. In running FreeWater, the single most common thing we deal with is advertisers we have to turn away or wrestle with for reasons that have nothing to do with interest. A small advertiser wants in but can't fill our minimum production run — so the deal dies on volume. Another advertiser wants the whole bottle to themselves and won't share the surface with anyone, which gets expensive fast. And plenty of advertisers would honestly rather just buy a digital screen, because that's the format they already understand and purchase every day. A digital dispenser quietly solves all three. There's no minimum order quantity on a digital ad slot — a small advertiser can buy one screen, in one location, for one afternoon. There's no realestate-sharing problem, because the screen is entirely theirs during their slot. And it meets advertisers exactly where they already live, in the world of digital billboards they know how to buy. Now, the honest hedge, because I won't overclaim this. I was never willing to use plastic bottles with FreeWater, so I can't tell you from direct experience whether a digital dispenser scales better than a well-designed bottle — a big square bottle like a Fiji could well scale comparably. What I can tell you is that for the specific job of getting advertisers to yes quickly and without friction, the dispenser removes obstacles the physical product simply can't. If I were starting over today, knowing how many good advertisers we turn away for being below our minimums or wanting the whole surface, I would give the digital dispenser very serious thought as a lead product, not a fallback. The rest is straightforward. You'll need a simple backend where advertisers upload their content, pick their dispenser locations and time slots, watch their campaign, pay, and go. You can route a portion of every ad sale — say ten percent — to a clean-water project, a school, or medical aid, which makes the whole thing negatively priced. And if you ever layer in follow-up marketing, do it the honest way we covered with the big brands: only for people who opt in through your app, where someone who chooses to scan or sign up can get a matching offer afterward. That consent-first version is the only version worth building — it keeps faith with everything the Honest Data chapter stood for, and it's the line you don't cross.

### The pure-software path: an app that turns attention into value

If you've got strong software engineers, you can go even lighter — no product, no machine, nothing physical at all. You build an app that pays people for their time, attention, and actions, and ties that value back to real goods and services. This is exactly the Generated-Value model from the vending chapter — the one where the user's participation, rather than the packaging, funds everything — built as a standalone app. It works in two moves. First, people earn credits by doing things worth something: watching ad videos, creating content or reviews, taking surveys, "time banking" by helping out in their community, referring friends, completing branded missions. Every action earns credits, and the credits are transparently pegged to local currency — a hundred credits to a dollar, say — so there's no guesswork and no mystery, which is what builds trust. Second, once they've earned enough, they spend those credits as value wherever digital payment is accepted — online stores, physical spots that take prepaid cards or QR payments, and yes, cryptocurrency wallets if you choose to integrate them, though crypto is just one option among several, not the point. Your app is the exchange layer turning attention into spendable value, and you can take a small fee on transactions as a revenue stream. The result can be genuinely powerful: the goal is that a person with no job and no money can watch a few ads, make a video that takes off, earn credits, and use them to order food, catch a bus, or buy clothes. Done right, that's not just an ad network — it's a small economic engine that rewards people for their time and voice, and if you route some of the ad revenue to charity, it's a fully digital negatively priced model with no factory anywhere in sight. One serious caution, the same one I gave for this model in the vending chapter, because it has real legal edges. The moment your credits become spendable as real money across many businesses, you're touching financial regulations — moneytransmission rules, identity requirements, and more — and that's especially true if you integrate crypto. A simple closed loop and a full open currency are very different risk levels. Build the vision, but get proper legal and compliance advice before you let credits become money. Don't improvise that part.

### The community-impact path: go nonprofit

There's a third route that drops advertising entirely, and in heavily regulated places it's sometimes the cleanest path of all: build it as a nonprofit, focused purely on impact and community. This pays off something from early in the book — that a nonprofit is often a more forgiving on-ramp — and it genuinely works. The first version is a Free Store. You register as a nonprofit, secure a space (often rent-free or discounted thanks to your status), and stock it with donated goods — grocery overstock, brand excess inventory, produce from farmers and distributors, contributions from local people and organizations. Volunteers or part-time staff run it. And here's the elegant part: you offer an optional subscription, say twenty dollars a month for unlimited access. Plenty of people in the community will choose to pay even though they don't have to, simply because they want to support the mission — while anyone who can't pay still gets what they need. Others donate more, offer services, or spread the word. It's part store, part movement, and entirely human. I've seen versions of this serve thousands of families, and I've seen others run hyper-locally out of a garage.

The second version decentralizes that same idea into a Free Fridge network. You place community refrigerators in trusted spots around town — outside local shops, churches, libraries, schools — stock them with donated fresh food and put a simple sign on each: "Take what you need, leave what you can." Volunteers, donors, and local businesses "adopt" individual fridges and keep them running. This isn't hypothetical — it's a real and growing movement. Community-fridge networks operate across many cities today, from Chicago's Love Fridge to dozens of fridges in Philadelphia and Richmond and beyond, all run by volunteers under that exact "take what you need" philosophy. It's powerful, it's visual, and anyone can understand it in a sentence. These nonprofit approaches work for reasons worth naming. They build immediate trust and goodwill. They're genuinely hard to criticize — you're not asking anyone to buy anything, you're inviting them to join something. They flex to any size. They plug straight into the existing rivers of food waste we've talked about, turning surplus into meals. And they can qualify for local, national, or international funding that a for-profit never could. That last point matters: where advertising is hard, grants and donations can fund the whole thing.

### Mix the building blocks

Here's the freeing truth to end on: you don't have to pick just one of these. Some of the strongest projects blend them. Run a nonprofit store that also shows digital ads to cover costs. Pair a subscription fridge network with app-based volunteer rewards and charitable tracking. Operate as a nonprofit for the credibility and tax advantages, use advertising for recurring revenue, add a "pay if you can" subscription for those who want to give, layer in app-based credits for engagement, and lean on sponsorships, grants, or local government programs to fill the gaps. There is no one-size-fits-all model, and you don't need one. You need the combination that fits your country, your people, and your mission. Think of all of these — the dispenser, the credits app, the free store, the free fridge — as building blocks. Mix them, match them, test them, and evolve until you find the structure that creates the most impact where you are. The factory was never the only door in. It was just the first one we happened to walk through. Next, let's talk about what happens when this whole model reaches the scale it's truly capable of.

## Chapter 22: A Sold Bottle Has a Ceiling. A Free One Doesn't

You thought you were giving away free water. It turns out you were building a new economy. Let's step back from the bottles and the trucks and the dispensers and ask the big question: how large is this free and negatively priced world, really, compared to the for-sale system it grows alongside? I'll be honest with you right away — nobody can give you an exact number, because this economy doesn't fully exist yet. But I can show you why it's dramatically larger, and once you see the mechanism, the scale speaks for itself.

### One transaction becomes many

Here's the engine of the whole thing. A regular bottle of water sells once for a dollar. You drink it, you toss it, and the money stops moving. That dollar did its one job and the transaction is over. Now take that same bottle and make it free. Suddenly it isn't the end of a transaction — it's the start of many. It can show you a relevant ad. It can donate to a cause. It can hand you a discount code. It can unlock a mobile app, or a whole new kind of app store sitting on top of physical products. It can enter you in a giveaway, drive you to a store, let you buy something with a scan, prompt you to tell a friend, start a conversation online, teach you something, and quietly collect honest data the whole time. One bottle stops being a single sale and becomes a doorway to ten interactions, or fifty, each one a piece of economic activity that simply did not exist in the old model. You didn't make the bottle more expensive. You turned a dead-end transaction into a living one.

### A sold bottle has a ceiling. A free one doesn't.

This points at something worth sitting with, because it's the real reason the math changes. A sold bottle's value is capped — it's worth its price, one dollar, and not a penny more. That's the ceiling, and it's fixed. A free bottle has no fixed ceiling at all. Most free bottles generate a small amount of value through their ad and engagement layers — a few cents, a few dollars. But every so often, a single bottle does something a sold one never could: someone scans its code, and that scan leads to a high-value purchase — a premium service, a big-ticket item, a click on one of those insurance or legal keywords that can be worth enormous sums — and that one bottle generates hundreds of dollars, or in a rare case, even a thousand. Let me be careful here, because I don't want you to misread it: that thousanddollar bottle is a rare outlier, not what a typical bottle does. The point isn't the number. The point is that there is no ceiling. A sold bottle can only ever be worth its price. A free bottle sits at the bottom of an open staircase, usually generating a little, occasionally generating a lot, with nothing capping the top. When you multiply an entire economy of products by "no fixed ceiling" instead of "capped at the price," you start to feel why the size question gets so large so fast.

### The system we have now runs on waste

It helps to see what this is replacing, because the current for-sale system is built on waste from end to end — the 20,000-mile bottle from two chapters ago, the warehouses, the freight damaged in transit, the third of all food that spoils before it's ever sold, the advertising that misses the people it was meant to reach. Add it all up and it's billions, probably trillions, of dollars in pure waste — and it's rarely questioned, because that's how it's always been done.

Free unlocks a smarter system — and the pieces multiply each other Everything in this book has been describing the replacement, and here's the part that makes the whole worth more than the sum of its parts: the pieces don't just add together, they compound. Consecutive delivery drops product house after house, cutting fuel and time and cost. Negatively priced freight means the trucks, drivers, and maintenance get funded by the ads on the vehicle, so shipping pays for itself. Personalized packaging turns every item into a billboard and a shopping portal. Honest data reveals what people actually want instead of what they could afford that day. And on-demand local production erases the need for the giant warehouses entirely.

Now watch how they reinforce each other. Honest data makes the packaging ads more valuable, because they're better targeted. Better-targeted ads fund more of the freight, pushing it further past zero. Negatively priced freight makes consecutive delivery more profitable, which lets you serve more homes, which generates more honest data — and around it goes, each piece making every other piece worth more. That compounding loop is why this economy isn't just a little bigger than a list of efficiencies. It's a system where the value of the whole climbs faster than any single part. This is the number line from the start of this book — the move from right of zero to left of zero — scaled up from a single bottle to an entire economy.

### So how big is it? Bigger — here's why

Honestly? It's impossible to calculate precisely, and I'd be lying if I gave you a confident figure for something that's still being built. So instead of a number, let me give you the reasons it's larger, and you can draw your own conclusion. You're unlocking entirely new ad revenue. You're producing honest data no one else on earth has. You're opening the door to affiliate sales on top of everyday goods. You're stripping waste out of the supply chain. You're adding an app-store layer to food and beverages that never existed. And you're replacing slow, lossy, old infrastructure with direct-to-home efficiency. Stack all of that up, and you're not looking at a system that's a few percent better. You're looking at one that could plausibly be many times more valuable than what we have today. I won't pretend to know the multiple, because no one can yet. What I'll tell you with confidence is the direction, and the direction is dramatically up.

### Why it really wins

But here's the thing I most want you to take from this chapter, because size alone was never the argument. This economy doesn't win simply because it's bigger. It wins because it's bigger and better — more efficient and more humane at the same time. It does more with less. It cuts the waste, the fuel, the spoilage, and the missed connections, and in the same motion it feeds people, funds causes, and hands real value back to the person at the end of the chain. A system that's both more profitable and more generous doesn't stay a niche for long.

So no, I can't hand you a precise figure for how big this gets. But you don't need one. You only need to see that a free bottle starts more transactions than a sold one ever could, that its ceiling is unbounded where the old one was capped, and that every piece of this model makes every other piece more valuable. Put that together and the size takes care of itself. You weren't just giving things away. You were building the economy that comes next — and it's far larger, and far kinder, than the one it replaces. Next, let's talk about where all of this is ultimately headed.

## Chapter 23: The App Store Layer

Once you've built a successful free or negatively priced product company, you might assume the innovation is finished. It isn't. There's a hidden layer sitting on top of everything you've built — one that turns your product into a platform. We call it the App Store Layer, and to understand why it's so powerful, you first have to understand when a company earns the right to build one at all.

### When is an app store warranted?

App stores don't appear randomly. They show up under one specific condition: when a company controls a product or service that people can't live without and use constantly. That's what gave Apple the right to build the App Store — not cleverness, but the fact that the iPhone is something people touch hundreds of times a day and genuinely can't function without. That constant, essential, daily contact is the thing that makes a platform layer make sense, because it's what guarantees people will actually keep showing up to the store you build on top. This is a general law of platforms, not a quirk of phones. Social networks earn the same layer for the same reason — people can't stop using them, so they become ecosystems with their own marketplaces of apps and tools built on top. Anywhere you find constant, essential, repeated attention, you eventually find a platform built on it. The attention is the foundation. The store is what gets built once the foundation is solid.

### Free essentials meet that bar — maybe better than anything

Now look at what you've built through that lens. Water. Food. Energy. Freight. These aren't things people touch hundreds of times a day by habit — they're things people literally cannot survive without. A free essential is, if anything, more "can't live without" than a smartphone. Which means a free-essentials company has the same claim to a platform layer that Apple has, for the exact same structural reason: you've established constant, daily, essential contact with many people. That reframes the Apple comparison entirely. The point isn't the grandiose "we're going to be like Apple." The point is precise and defensible: you satisfy the same condition Apple satisfied, the same condition social networks satisfy. You control something people can't live without and engage with constantly. And that condition — not Apple's brand, not its market cap — is what earns the right to build a marketplace on top. You've earned it the moment your free essential reaches people every day.

### One door becomes a hallway of doors

What does that earned position unlock? Think about what your product does right now: it delivers a free physical item, a printed ad or QR code, and a link to something digital. Today, that link is a single door — it goes to one place and does one thing. The App Store Layer turns that same scannable surface into a hallway of doors. One scan, many destinations, and the menu can change based on who's scanning, where they are, and when. It's the App Store on your phone — except it launches from a water bottle, a box of cereal, or a vending machine, and it updates in real time. This is the digital echo of the idea from the last chapter: just as a free bottle turns one transaction into many, the App Store Layer turns one scan into many possibilities. And from this earned position, the ceiling on what can live behind that scan is as high as the web itself. Anything that exists online or in an app store can sit there: a daily lottery where it's legal, shopping, free samples from other brands, games, services, charity tools, entertainment. The reason "as limitless as the web" doesn't read as hype is that you earned it — you're not claiming any random product deserves a platform; you're claiming that because you control an essential people use daily, you've met the same bar every major platform met, and from there the possibilities really are that broad.

### Why this is powerful: it runs the whole model

Here's the part that matters most, because the App Store Layer isn't just a menu of fun things to scan — it's the connective tissue that ties the entire digital half of this book together. Almost everything you've learned runs through this layer. The location-based ad targeting from the packaging chapter? It's delivered here. The honest data from a few chapters back? Captured here, with consent. The affiliate sale that gives a single bottle no ceiling? It happens here. The charity-voting tool that lets users choose where their donation goes — one of the most powerful and on-mission things you can offer — lives here. The App Store Layer is the surface where the targeting, the data, the revenue, and the mission all actually come to life. That's why it's not a bonus feature. It's the operating system for the digital side of everything you've built. It helps to think about what goes inside it by purpose rather than as a random pile. Some of it exists to give the user value — samples, discounts, a daily lottery, genuinely useful apps — which is what keeps people scanning. Some of it deepens the mission — the charity voting, letting people direct the good they're doing. And some of it generates revenue and data — affiliate shopping, targeted offers, the things that fund the whole machine. As for the biggest possibilities, the music and movies and shows, those are where this could go at full scale through partnerships down the road — not something a young company hosts on day one, but a direction the layer can grow toward once it's earned its audience.

### It gets better the more it's used

There's a loop here worth naming, because it's what makes this a true platform and not just a clever menu. Once people trust the package, they scan it more often. The more they scan, the more you learn about what's genuinely useful to them, and the more relevant the value you can offer back. That relevance builds more trust, which earns more scans — and around it goes. The App Store Layer gets better the more it's used, which is exactly the property that separates a platform from a product. A static link never improves. This compounds. And it does so honestly. The layer learns what's working not by surveilling anyone, but because users choose to engage with it and get real value in return. This is the contributed-not-extracted principle from the Honest Data chapter, applied directly: people aren't being tracked in the background, they're opting into something that gives back, and what you learn is something they chose to share. That's the line that keeps this powerful without becoming the kind of data harvesting the rest of this book stands against. This experience gets even stronger when the surface isn't a static bottle but a smart machine. A vending machine or smart fridge that already knows who's standing in front of it — the targeting models from the vending chapter — can personalize the App Store Layer in real time to that specific person, serving the right doors before they even scan. The platform and the machine reinforce each other.

### You don't need this to start

Let me be clear, the way I have been about every frontier idea in this book: you absolutely do not need any of this to begin. The App Store Layer is advanced. It's the thing the technical founder graduates into once the basics are working and the trust is built — not the thing you launch with. But the founders who keep going will eventually want it, because this is precisely how a free product company becomes a free ecosystem. It's the same pattern that turned a phone into a platform and a social network into an economy, applied to the essentials people can't live without. And it's one more reason the free economy doesn't just match the old one — it outgrows it, layer by layer. Next, let's look at where all of this is ultimately heading.

## Chapter 24: Beyond Free — The Circular and the Ad-Free

If you've made it this far, you already know the central surprise of this book: giving things away for free, or even paying people to take them, isn't just possible — it can be more profitable than selling the same goods the old way. But there's further to climb. If you want the most efficient, most profitable, and most sustainable version of all of this, you have to go beyond free-with-ads — and there are two ways up. You can make the whole system circular, which is radical efficiency. Or you can drop the ads entirely, which is radical ethics. The summit is where both meet. Let me take you up each path.

### Path one: make it circular

The first way beyond free-with-ads is to stop throwing the product away. Use durable packaging — recyclable glass or something similarly long-lived — for as many products as you can. Then collect the empties the same way you delivered them: on consecutive routes, house after house, back to a local facility. There you strip off the old label, clean and sterilize the bottle, apply a fresh design, refill it, and send it back out locally. Same bottle, same route, brand-new cycle. And it isn't just bottles — it's an entire ecosystem that loops forever, right in your own neighborhood. Everything localizes: the design, the ad printing, the filling and sealing, the delivery route, the collection, and the honest data that coordinates all of it. Here's the economic engine underneath: each loop costs less than the one before, because you're no longer manufacturing the bottle, only refilling it. The single most expensive part of the product — making the container — happens once and then earns its keep over and over. So reuse compounds the economics the same way every other piece of this model compounds; the loop gets cheaper and more profitable each time around. This is the answer to a system that's genuinely broken. Traditional recycling barely works — by most estimates less than 10% of plastic is ever actually recycled, and much of what is gets downcycled into something lesser or shipped across the world to become someone else's problem. But when you control the loop and keep it local, you escape all of that. This isn't recycling. It's reinvention. The same bottle comes back as a clean slate, ready to carry a new ad, a new message, a new donation — a living product that earns, gives, and performs better the more it moves. And when the loop is tight enough, the negative pricing can go remarkably deep: the product can be free, donate to charity, pay you to consume it, pay the company to deliver it, and even help pay the manufacturer to make it, all at once. I'll be careful with the exact figures, because how deep you can push depends entirely on your costs and scale — but the direction is unmistakable, and the deeper you reuse, the further left of zero the whole thing can travel.

### Path two: drop the ads entirely

Now the second path, and I want to be honest with you about something personal here. This whole book has been built on advertising — using the packaging as ad space to fund everything. That model works, and it was the right way for me to start, because it could be proven with modest money. But it was never the purest version. If I'd had real capital when I began, I might have launched a different kind of business entirely — one that pays people without any ads or data at all. We call it Everyone's Economy, and it's the most ethical economic model I know of, because it monetizes nobody. It just runs on efficiency so extreme that the system can afford to give value away and still come out ahead. The clearest real, present-day example is sitting on rooftops right now: solar panels. Install them, and over ten or twenty years they pay themselves off by offsetting your electricity bill. At the moment they break even, the system is officially free. And from then on, it pays you — clean energy with real value, and not a single ad or piece of personal data involved. That's a negatively priced product powered by pure efficiency, and the same logic can scale to power whole communities. Now look where this is heading, and I'll flag clearly that this part is the future, not today. As automation matures, you might own a self-driving car, a robot, or an AI system — and when you're not using it, it goes out and gives rides, delivers goods, or performs services on your behalf. Over time it pays off its own cost, and after that, it pays you. No attention monetized, no privacy sold — just extreme efficiency turned into income. That points at the principle beneath this entire path: you don't always need to add new revenue to cross zero. Sometimes cutting waste to near-nothing is enough. If you design a business with near-zero marginal cost — renewable energy, automation, smart design — you can deliver value so cheaply that you can afford to pay people to use it freely and still profit.

### The summit: where both paths meet

Here's the mountaintop, and it's the view the whole book has been climbing toward. These two paths aren't rivals — they're two halves of one destination. Imagine a system that is local and circular and near-zero-cost all at once: products made and reused in your own community, looping forever at falling cost, powered by clean energy and automation, paying people to participate — with advertising now an optional layer you might not even need. That's the most efficient, most ethical, and most sustainable form of everything in this book. The ads were the onramp. This is the destination.

### What this actually means

Let me ground the big claims, because they matter too much to leave floating. A system like this can out-compete the old economy on its own terms — on price, on efficiency, on profit — while doing dramatically more good along the way. That's the real claim, and it's a defensible one: not a slogan about defeating capitalism, but a model that simply wins the contest the old system claims to care about, and then keeps going past where the old system stops, into giving. And the human effect of it resembles something like a basic income — but one that emerges from efficiency rather than from taxes, policy, or redistribution. Value flows to people because the systems around them became cheap enough to share, not because anyone voted to hand it out. Same outcome, entirely different mechanism.

### The invitation

I'll leave you with the idea that excites me most, because it's where the biggest opportunities of the next era are hiding. If you invent a product or service that only makes sense in a world where things are free or negatively priced — something that couldn't have turned a profit on the right side of zero, but works beautifully on the left — then you've built something that simply could not have existed before. That's the real frontier of the number line we started with: not just moving existing products across zero, but creating entirely new things that live there natively. That's how you get genuinely wealthy while paying both companies and consumers to save money. You win. They win. And the world wins too. This is how a system feeds people, informs them, rewards them, and puts the power of manufacturing and distribution back into the hands of communities — all while turning a profit and healing the planet in the process. It's not charity, and it's not a fantasy. It's the furthest, cleanest expression of one simple move: crossing zero. Next, a short detour — the one chapter in this book you can act on with twelve dollars and a credit card.

## Chapter 25: The Free.com Domain Name Gold Rush

A quick note before we start: this is the one chapter in the book that isn't really about building your company. Everything else — the manufacturing, the advertisers, the delivery, the data — is core to the playbook. This is a side opportunity the free economy creates around the edges, an optional, asymmetric bet you can place on the future the rest of this book describes. It's also the only chapter where you can participate with about twelve dollars and a credit card instead of a factory. So read it as a bonus, not a pillar — and with that framing set, let me tell you why I find it genuinely exciting. Here's my confession, which doubles as my argument: I personally own more than 500 domain names that start with the word "free." I didn't buy them as a hobby. I bought them because I'm convinced they're some of the most undervalued digital real estate available — and I was willing to put my own money behind that belief, 500 times over. So this isn't a theory I'm floating. It's a bet I've already made, repeatedly. Whether it pays off depends entirely on whether the free economy actually takes off the way this book argues — and I want to be honest about that uncertainty from the start. You've probably heard of someone who got rich buying a domain early. Cars.com sold for hundreds of millions of dollars — and that was just a single word. But let me puncture that example before you get too excited by it, because it's exactly the kind of story that misleads people. For every Cars.com, there are thousands of domains that never became worth a cent, and most domains never will. Domain investing is survivorship bias in its purest form: you hear about the handful that hit and never about the millions that didn't. So I'm not promising you a Cars.com. I'm pointing at the same underlying fact those rare wins prove — that a clear, valuable domain can become worth a fortune — and asking what happens when you apply it to the word "free" in a world steadily moving toward free and negatively priced goods.

### Why AI might make these more valuable, not less

I know the obvious objection, because it's the first thing smart people raise: aren't domains becoming obsolete? In a world of AI assistants, voice search, and smart devices, who even types a web address anymore? This is the most important question in the chapter, so let me give you my honest reasoning — not as a prediction, but as the thesis I'm betting on. I think AI may not eliminate the need for a destination so much as sharpen it. When someone tells their assistant, “Find me the best free coffee nearby," that request still must be routed somewhere — to some source that represents the answer. And it's at least plausible that a clear, exact-match destination like FreeCoffee.com becomes a more legible place to send that request, not a less relevant one. In that scenario the domain isn't competing with the AI; it's a candidate for the answer the AI returns. The more our searches shift from typed keywords toward natural, spoken, intent-driven requests — "free vegan food near me," "free EV charging on my route" — the more I suspect a domain that exactly matches that intent could gain value, because it's about as precise a match for the request as exists. I won't overstate it: AI may not bury these domains, and it may move the best exact-match domains closer to the front of the line. That's the bet. The logic is sound to me, but it's a bet, not a guarantee.

### They're gateways, not just websites

What makes me most optimistic is that these aren't merely websites in the old sense — they can function as gateways, which is what makes them potentially far more valuable than a static page. A domain like FreeEnergy.com could become the homepage of a free or negatively priced marketplace. It could be the front door to one of those App Store Layers we talked about — the platform that launches from a scannable product. It could be the anchor an AI assistant or voice search routes people to, a portal into a game world the way Roblox uses named portals, or an entry point into augmented and mixed reality. Even generating nothing today, the right domain could be the anchor that launches a real business tomorrow. And here's the scarcity that makes it behave like real estate: there is only one FreeCoffee.com. Only one FreeRent.com. Only one FreeEnergy.com. Paired with a real platform someday, a front door like that can't be duplicated by anyone.

### What to look for

What makes a "free" domain worth considering is that it names something people would genuinely want for free, in a category where the free model can actually work — the Embedded Value and Generated Value categories from earlier in the book. So you look at basic needs: food, water, shelter, energy, medicine. Lifestyle upgrades: free education, AI tools, mental health support. Experiences: games, live events, fitness, travel. And localizations, which are easy to overlook and often wide open — FreeMexicanFood.com, free-anything in a specific language or region. Anything that clearly falls into something that could become free or negatively priced is, potentially, a front door worth claiming.

### Be honest with yourself: this is a bet

Let me reinforce the honest framing, because I'd rather you go in clear-eyed than chase this on hype. This is genuinely speculative, and it sits at the speculative edge of everything in this book. These domains only pay off if the free and negatively priced economy actually arrives the way I've argued it will. I believe it will — I've staked 500 domains and a book on that belief — but believing isn't knowing, and most individual domains, including plenty of mine, may never become worth anything. You don't need to build a business behind every domain you buy, and you absolutely should not expect every one to hit; the honest expectation is that most won't, and the thesis only works if a few do. What you're really doing is what the early internet pioneers did: buying digital land before the map is filled in, while it's cheap and the rules are still being written. They didn't know which addresses would matter either. They bought territory and waited.

### The keys to the city

Here's how I think about my 500 domains, and how I'd frame grabbing a few of your own — as a small, optional flier, not a core strategy. If you believe in free and negatively priced business models, then these domains are, at most, keys to a city that hasn't been built yet. If this economy takes off, some of these may turn out to be front doors people walk through to find it — and there's exactly one of each. The map is still mostly empty and the land is still cheap. I've planted my flags, knowing most may never matter and a few just might. You might want to plant a couple of your own while you still can — with money you can afford to treat as a bet. ## Chapter 26: Seven Projects — And How to Get Them Off the Ground

When the first edition of this book went out into the world, something happened I didn't fully expect. My inbox, my DMs, and my LinkedIn filled up with messages from readers in dozens of countries — high schoolers and retirees, college students and factory owners, activists, nonprofit founders, even a few executives. They didn't just want to say they enjoyed the book. They wanted to build. And they kept asking the same three questions: "Where do I start with this exact product?" "How would this work in my city?" "What's the very first step?" So, I rolled up my sleeves and helped them map it out, one project and one first step at a time. This chapter is the result: seven real example projects, the kind I've personally worked through with readers just like you, broken down so you can take an idea and actually get it off the ground. But before the first project, two things will make all seven make sense — the pattern they all share, and the single most important decision you'll make. The pattern every project shares Whatever product you choose, a free product company follows the same core sequence. Here it is, tight, as your reference: 1. Pick a product or service people use every day. 2. Choose your model: free or negatively priced. 3. Choose your structure — nonprofit or for-profit — and your donation amount if any (a minimum of roughly 2¢ to 10¢ per unit). 4. Lock in a descriptive name, the matching domain, and the social handles — the same day. 5. Design the packaging across its three layers: printed design, a smart QR code or logo anchor, and an optional augmented-reality layer if possible. (Or, for a dispenser or vending model, the digital screen.)

6. Build a simple website. 7. Build a movement online before you're ready — start posting immediately, don't wait for everything to be perfect. 8. Fund it — through donations and volunteers, or through advertisers. 9. Distribute: in person, vending machines, direct-to-consumer, B2B, or in stores. 10. Measure engagement and data, then improve and repeat. That's the skeleton. Every project below is this exact sequence applied to a different product — so once you see it drive a bakery, you'll recognize it driving all seven.

### The most important decision: start as a nonprofit if you can

Before any specific project, the single most valuable piece of advice I can give you, especially if this is your first build: if your country legally allows nonprofits, that is almost always the easiest and fastest way to launch. I know that surprises people, since most of this book focused on the for-profit, ad-supported model — that's the path I took with FreeWater. But as I said back in the chapter on picking your vehicle, of every model in this book, the nonprofit route lets the most of every dollar and every hour go straight to the mission, because so little leaks out along the way. Here's why it's so powerful, in short. It runs on donations and volunteers instead of ads — which means you don't need a single advertiser to start, you don't have to sell ad space, and you don't have to close one deal. You just need a cause people believe in. It unlocks tax-deductible donations, grants, and donated product from brands sitting on excess inventory — and people give more freely, and companies hand over product more readily, when there's a charitable mission and a tax writeoff attached. And it's the easiest path if you don't have a business or advertising background: the for-profit ad model effectively requires you to master the ad industry, a craft that can take years, while the nonprofit model lets you skip that climb entirely and focus on something far more natural — telling a story people want to support.

The one real tradeoff, as I mentioned earlier, is that a pure nonprofit gives up the ability to raise money by selling shares and to one day sell the company. But here's the part most people miss: starting as a nonprofit doesn't lock you in. It's the ideal place to learn the ropes — you'll figure out manufacturing and distribution, build real relationships with co-packers and suppliers and distributors, and prove the model works, all with far less pressure than a for-profit startup. Then, once you understand the machine from the inside, you can add a for-profit arm later, from a position of real strength. This isn't unusual: plenty of well-known nonprofits run commercial ventures to fund their missions — Newman's Own, the food brand, gives 100% of its profits to charity. So you're never choosing one path forever. You're just choosing where to start. Nearly every project below can launch as a nonprofit first, and the door to going commercial stays wide open the whole time. One note so I don't repeat myself seven times: every project starts the same first way — a descriptive, searchable name, the matching domain and handles locked in the same day, and a movement built online from day one. I'll say that once, here, and then each project will jump straight to what makes it unique. Now let's build.

### Project #1: The Free Bakery

Let's start simple, human, and cheap. Free bread, free sandwiches, whatever your community needs most. If you get good at social media, you can get this off the ground for roughly a thousand dollars, depending on where you live. This one's also our template — I'll walk it fully, and the later projects will lean on what we establish here. The name and the paperwork. Pick something descriptive — Free Bakery, Free Bread, Free Sandwiches — so that when someone searches "free sandwich nonprofit" in your country, you're the obvious result. Before you fall in love with it, check that the .org or .com is available affordably and the handles are open across platforms. If everything's open, that's usually a good sign the name isn't trademarked either — not a guarantee, so do a quick trademark search if you have any doubt, but when it's all available you're probably clear. Lock it the same day. Then file your nonprofit paperwork. Be ready: this is usually the most expensive and slowest part, and it varies a lot by country. But you don't wait on your hands while it's pending.

Start the movement now. While your status is pending, buy some bread and sandwich materials, give them away, and record everything. Post multiple videos a day, and ask viewers to start handing out free bread too — you're not just feeding people, you're starting a movement. One safety note: if it's not safe to hand out food in your city center, take it to a university campus, where students are generous, social, and exactly the audience that shares this. You already know the playbook from the social-media chapters: post consistently, lead with the mission, let the people you help tell the story, repost your best. Turn on the flywheel. When your nonprofit status kicks in, add a "Donate Now" link to your bios. Now every video feeds people and invites support. The donations start small — that's normal. You buy more bread, distribute more, film it all; every loaf becomes more content, and more content brings more donations. Once you've collected enough, pay yourself a small, honest salary — say, roughly a thousand a month to start — and keep buying in larger bulk, because bigger orders mean a lower cost per loaf and more people fed per dollar. And get a kitchen without buying one: approach ghost kitchens or existing bakeries and ask to use their space during off-hours as a donation to your cause. Many will say yes — it makes them look good and costs them almost nothing. Do you need ads? No. Down the road you might print ads on your bags and wrappers for extra revenue, and that's fine — but the key difference from the forprofit path is you don't need them. You've already built a sustainable engine out of donations. The ads, if you ever add them, are a bonus, not the foundation. And when you're ready, you can launch a for-profit arm alongside the nonprofit — from enormous strength, because you'll already have the volume discounts (usable in the for-profit arm), the supplier and kitchen relationships, an audience that trusts you, and hard-won experience including every mistake you already made on lower stakes. That's far cheaper and less risky than launching a for-profit bakery on day one, where every mistake comes straight out of your own pocket. The for-profit version (the vending route). Maybe you've got a business background, or you simply want a company you can scale and sell. The first steps are identical — descriptive name, domain, handles, build the movement. What changes is how you pay for it: with no donations or volunteers, you need a model that funds itself from the start, and the cleanest for a bakery is the vending route. Use the smart vending machines from the vending chapter — the ones with a digital ad screen on the front — placed in high-traffic spots, and sell the screen space to cover the food, the machine, and your profit, then stack on printed wrapper ads and a mobile app with offers and rewards. One free sandwich, multiple revenue streams. The catch: this isn't free to start — machines and software take real capital, so unless you're independently wealthy you'll raise from investors, which means a team. Your most important hire is an ad-industry executive who's sold this kind of space before and already has advertiser relationships. You'll also want a strong software developer (or a serious "vibe coder") and a social-media lead if that's not you. The honest truth, the one I keep returning to: in the for-profit model, every mistake costs you money — no donations to absorb a bad month, no volunteers working free — which is exactly why, if this is your first build, I still say start as a nonprofit. But with the experience, the team, and the stomach for it, the for-profit version scales further and faster, and it's yours to keep.

### Project #2: Free Water

The product that started it all for me. Everything from the bakery still applies — descriptive name, domain, handles, movement — and nonprofit is still easiest where it's allowed. Water actually makes the nonprofit path simpler: funded by donations, you need no ads at all, and you hand out whatever water you can source. But many of you will want the ad-supported FreeWater model, and with water you've got two paths — a dispenser or bottles — and which is easier depends heavily on where you live. The dispenser route. If you live somewhere with a valuable ad industry, expensive labor, and high fuel prices, building bottles from scratch is brutal early on. There, I'd point you to the dispenser model from the chapter on building without a factory: a giant vending machine wired to the city's tap supply that filters and purifies water and dispenses it free without bottles, with a digital ad screen on the front. Why it's so much easier in a high-cost country: advertisers can buy in cheaply, big budget or small — someone can pay around fifty dollars to play their video on one machine, or run it across your whole network. That low entry point is nearly impossible with bottles early on, because outsourced manufacturers won't let you order a small enough quantity — the minimum order alone can sink you before you've sold an ad. A dispenser sidesteps that entirely: the water's from the city, not a factory, and your inventory is digital, so you can sell a tiny slice to anyone. Master one machine, get it fully advertiser-funded, then scale by adding machines — put a map on your site, let advertisers click any machine or all of them and book in real time, and your website quietly becomes a self-serve ad platform. The bottle route. If you're committed to actual bottles, the honest truth is the easiest start is plastic, simply because it's so cheap to manufacture — especially a bottle shaped and sized like a 500ml Fiji bottle, so you can tap existing molds and supply chains. And you can still make every bottle donate; something like The Ocean Cleanup works beautifully. I know some of you are thinking but it's plastic. You're right. But hold onto this principle: solve one problem at a time. Start with the biggest one — the cost to the consumer — get free water into people's hands and build your movement, and then roll out better packaging (aluminum, glass, paper cartons) as your volume and budget grow. Don't let perfect be the enemy of started. A second line: water people pay for. Once your brand is out there, people will reach out wanting the same thing but to sell — they already sell water and don't want to undercut themselves. So, consider a second line: ad-supported water that retailers can sell. Whether it also donates is up to you, but here's why it's worth it. I've seen teams in India build large businesses this way, going shop to shop convincing every store to stock their ad-supported bottles instead of the usual brands. Even though that water isn't free to the end customer, it dramatically increases your total volume with your manufacturer — and more volume means a lower cost per unit, which you can pass along to make your free water's ad space even cheaper, or keep as healthier margins. Your paid line ends up strengthening your free one.

### Project #3: The Free Grocery Store

Of every project here, this is the one where I won't pretend it's close: by far the easiest way to pull it off is the nonprofit model. The foundation is the same, but this one leans on the nonprofit structure harder than anything. We touched on it with the Free Store Model earlier; here's how to actually launch one. The ideal setup. Three things carry the whole operation. Your physical location is donated, so you pay no rent — usually a store's single biggest expense. Volunteers provide the workforce, so labor stays near zero. And the people who shop free are invited to pay an optional donation or join a simple subscription — and many will choose to give, even though they don't have to, because they believe in what you're building. And no, this isn't bad food: a lot of people picture expired cans and bruised produce, but it doesn't have to be that way at all. You collect product donations directly from top brands and local farmers glad to move excess inventory to a good cause (and earn the tax benefit), and you use cash donations to buy quality products to round out the shelves. The result is a real grocery store people are proud to shop in. Adding ad-supported goods, later and slowly. Once you're sustainable on donations and subscriptions, you can layer in ad-supported goods for more revenue — one product at a time, no rush, because the donations already keep the lights on. And here's a clever move most miss: you can often get the ad infrastructure itself donated. A company donates the digital screens, the printing, or the wrappers — as an in-kind donation to your nonprofit, they get the write-off, and you get the infrastructure free. Then you either sell that ad space for revenue or use it to advertise your own mission. Donated infrastructure, monetizable output — the kind of compounding efficiency only a nonprofit can pull off. The for-profit version. I'll be straight: a for-profit free grocery store is much harder to scale. Realistically, I believe the first ones at any real size will be built by a major ad agency or a company like Google or Facebook, because they already have the advertisers lined up to cover an entire store's worth of goods. Without that kind of advertiser firepower, you're making each product free one at a time, closing advertiser after advertiser, SKU by SKU. It can be done, but it's a long road — which is exactly why I'd steer almost everyone to the nonprofit version first.

### Project #4: Free Tampons & Pads

Menstrual products are one of the clearest things that should be free — and as a nonprofit, you don't even need ads. The whole model here is simple: get the products themselves donated. Major brands make these by the millions, and many will happily give to a cause built around dignity and menstrual equity — it's good for their image and they get the tax benefit. You collect the donated product, your volunteers distribute it, and that's the entire engine: no ad space to sell, no advertisers to chase. As I noted in the tampons discussion earlier, this is one of those products where the nonprofit route is often the smoothest of all. If you want more reach later, you can optionally add wall-mounted or standard vending machines with digital ad screens in schools, offices, gyms, and restrooms — but it's never necessary as a nonprofit, just extra reach and revenue if you want it. And remember the lesson from the packaging chapters: the tiny wrapper on a pad or tampon carries only a thin layer of ads and was never your main earner. The real ad revenue lives on the digital screens, your social media, and your website. The for-profit version mirrors the for-profit bakery — raise enough to set up your free vending network up front, list each machine on your site, and sell the screen space location by location, with advertisers covering everything while the product stays free. As always, the cost and risk sit on you, so for a first build the donationfunded nonprofit is the easier start.

### Project #5: Free Sunscreen

Sunscreen is one of those products people are genuinely happy to receive, and it makes some of the most fun, shareable content you can produce. As a nonprofit, collect donations, recruit volunteers, and take it straight where people need it: the main beaches in your city. Set up a station, or just have volunteers walk the beach offering a squirt or two — it's hard to imagine a more welcome gift on a hot day. And the content writes itself: film everything, then go a step further and ask a few of the people you just helped to record a quick video about why free sunscreen matters to them. Maybe they lost someone to skin cancer; maybe you just saved their afternoon. Those real human moments are exactly what spreads — growing your movement as it goes.

For a for-profit version, you'll likely want a vending machine with a digital billboard on the front to dispense individual servings, with the screen advertisers covering the sunscreen and your profit. From there, add the mobile-app layer we've discussed: the user opens the app, watches a short ad, earns a digital token, and the token unlocks a free serving from the machine. Now two ad streams work together — the billboard and the in-app ads — and the sunscreen stays free for whoever wants it.

### Project #6: The Free Bus

A free bus is one of the most visible, community-changing projects you can build — and unless you already have all the advertisers lined up for a for-profit model on day one, it should start as a nonprofit. Foundation's the same, so here's how to get the wheels turning. Eliminate your two biggest costs up front. On any transit project, the vehicle and the labor are the big ones, so aim to remove both: work to get a bus or van donated, and recruit volunteers to drive it. With a donated vehicle and a free driver, your only real recurring cost is fuel — and that's where the model takes over. Just like the trucks we discussed in the logistics chapter, your bus is a moving billboard driving the same streets daily in front of thousands of people. Wrap it in ads, little by little, and that revenue doesn't just cover the fuel — it covers the fuel and then some, giving you surplus to scale. Nail the basics first. Before anything fancy, lock in a specific, dependable route the community can count on — same stops, same times, every day. Reliability is what turns a free bus from a novelty into something people build their lives around, and it's what makes your ad space genuinely valuable. Then layer in new revenue slowly: your bus is constantly moving through the city, so it's a natural collector of mapping and road data, and it can handle the occasional product delivery along its route. Each stream makes the operation more sustainable and pushes you toward a bus line that doesn't just break even but generates surplus for the mission. The for-profit version runs into the familiar difference: no donated vehicle, no volunteer drivers, so you'll need to raise enough to pay for vehicles and drivers yourself — and keep paying — until self-driving technology matures enough to remove the driver. Once the buses drive themselves, that recurring labor cost disappears and the ad-supported model gets dramatically easier. Until then you're carrying payroll, which is why — unless you've already got the advertisers and capital lined up — the nonprofit route is the smoother place to begin.

### Project #7: The Free Cell Phone Carrier

For the last one, let's go big and go digital. A free or negatively priced phone carrier is different from everything else here — nothing to hand out on a beach, no vehicle to wrap. This one lives or dies on software. If the bakery was the easiest project in this book, consider this the most ambitious. Build the platform. At its core, this is the Generated-Value model from earlier in the book applied to phone service: ad-supported calls priced all the way down to free, or even negative. Advertisers pay to reach your users, and that revenue covers their calls and data. Play a short ad at the start of a call, or let users earn credit by engaging with ads in the app, and suddenly talk time isn't a bill — it's a reward. And the technology is the business here, so the app has to be three things at once: genuinely easy to use (people are switching from carriers they already have, so any friction costs you), scalable (the model only works at volume — a thousand users is a hobby, a million is a real ad business), and equipped with a clean backend where advertisers log in and upload their own content. That self-serve platform is what lets revenue scale without you closing every deal — the same principle as the clickable map of machines, applied to a phone network. Start smaller, then scale. Here's how to lower the risk: don't launch a full carrier on day one. Start with a negatively priced messaging and calling app that competes with something like WhatsApp — free to use, with, say, 10 to 20% of the ad revenue going to a cause. That alone is a compelling pitch: a messaging app that gives back every time you use it. It's far easier to build than a full carrier, it gets you users, and it proves your ad model works. Then, with a real user base and ad engine behind you, scale up into a full carrier — same users, same advertisers, bigger product. And you don't have to build the network itself: the realistic path is to partner with an existing carrier and resell their network — operating as what the industry calls an MVNO — so you focus all your energy on the app, the experience, and the advertiser platform. A word on the path. Of every project here, this is the one most likely to require real funding and a serious technical team — a top-notch developer or two, and someone who can sell ad space at scale, just like the for-profit bakery. But the encouraging part is that the bar is dropping fast: as AI keeps improving, building software like this gets easier and cheaper every year, so what used to take a large engineering team can increasingly be done by a small, scrappy one. It can absolutely be built to do good — even to donate with every call — but be honest about what it takes. This is a platform business, and platforms are built, not improvised.

## Chapter 27: The 20,000-Mile Bottle

Think back to where you started this book. You probably opened it suspecting, somewhere deep down, that "free" was a gimmick — that someone, somewhere, always has to pay, and that giving things away could never be a real business. Look at what you carry now. You understand the number line, and that zero was never the floor — that there's a whole world to the left of it. You know the User Test, and how to tell what is free from the kind that turns the person into the product. You know the packaging is an interface, the 10% Rule turns small change into rivers of giving, honest data is the truest signal a business has ever had, and consecutive delivery only becomes profitable once you reach zero or beyond it. You didn't just read a book. You walked in doubting free could work, and you're walking out holding the entire machine.

And here's what that means about you. A few years ago, this industry didn't exist. Today you understand how to build it — how to make a product free, make it profitable, scale it, fund it with ads and goodwill, and turn every single unit into a force for good. That puts you in rare company. Not most people. Not even most entrepreneurs. You are now one of the very few people on earth who knows how to do this. So let me tell you what you're really starting, because it's bigger than a company. You're starting something built to be copied. This is the part that took me years to understand, and it's the heart of everything: the goal was never to beat Coca-Cola, or out-muscle Google, or be the only one who gives things away. The goal was to prove that crossing zero works so undeniably that the giants have no choice but to follow. And the day they do — the day Pepsi and Coke are competing over who gives away more, the day a company like Google bolts its advertisers onto free essentials — that isn't the day you lose. That's the day you've won the only thing that ever mattered. A world that competes to give the most instead of taking the most. Being early was never about being alone. It was about lighting the way. So, get to work. Start small if you need to — pick one project from the last chapter, get a few products into a few hands, make a few videos, gather a few good people around you. If it works, scale it. If it doesn't, change the angle and test it again. Then keep going. Very few people have ever built what you're about to build, and that's exactly why you get to write the rules. I'm not going to promise you it'll work — anyone who promises you that is selling something, and you know better now. But I'll tell you what becomes possible if it does. Ten years from now, your free product could be on retail shelves, in backpacks and vending machines, in refugee camps and classrooms and on kitchen tables around the world — not just helping people, but quietly changing how everyone thinks about value, business, and generosity. I can't promise that future. But if it arrives, it will be because people exactly like you decided to build it. You have the playbook now. The future isn't just free — it's everything that opens up the moment you stop asking people to pay and start paying them back. So go to the left of zero. And go prove it.

## Chapter 28: The Ripple Effect

The emotional reach.
